These four forms are grouped together because they all ask one thing: find the rule hidden in a set of figures. What You Will Practice in the Figure Series, Classification, Analogy & Counting Chapter

Figure series

  • A shape turning 45°, 90° or 135° at each step, clockwise or anticlockwise
  • A dot or small shape moving round the corners or sides of the box
  • Sides or lines added one at a time, and shading that alternates or cycles
  • Five-figure series with two or three parts changing at once, and turns that grow each step (Level 2)

Figure classification (odd one out)

  • Three turned copies of one figure and a mirror image as the odd one
  • Dots inside a polygon linked to its number of sides — equal, one more, one fewer, or two fewer
  • An inner shape that is the same as the outer one, or has one side fewer

Figure analogy

  • One change from figure 1 to figure 2: a turn, a mirror or water image, shading reversed, inner and outer shapes swapping, or one more side (Level 1)
  • Two changes at once, where a wrong option makes only one of them (Level 2)

Counting of figures

  • Triangles in a triangle with lines from the vertex, a square with diagonals, and overlapping figures
  • Squares and rectangles in grids, with the formula shortcut where one applies
  • Wrong options that miss the large shapes or count one shape twice

The four forms, in pictures

Figure classificationIn (a), (b) and (c) the number of dots equals the number of sides: 3, 4 and 5. The hexagon in (d) has only 4 dots, so (d) is the odd one.
Figure analogyFrom figure 1 to figure 2 the inner and outer shapes swap places. So the answer is a circle with a small triangle inside it.
Counting of figuresTwo lines from the top vertex split the base into 3 parts. Triangles = 3 × 4 / 2 = 6: three small, two made of two parts, and the whole triangle.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Following the whole figure at once. A series usually has two or three parts with separate rules. Track one part across every figure, write its rule, then do the next part.
  • Taking a mirror image for a turned copy. A figure turned through any angle is still the same figure; a mirror image is not. In classification the mirror image is very often the odd one.
  • Missing the big shapes when counting. Count small pieces first, then shapes made of two, three and four pieces, and finally the whole figure. Most wrong options leave out one of these groups.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Figure Series, Classification, Analogy & Counting

Q1. In a series, an arrow points up in figure 1, to the top-right in figure 2 and to the right in figure 3. Where does it point in figure 4?
Each step turns the arrow 45° clockwise: up → top-right → right. One more 45° turn gives bottom-right. Answer: towards the bottom-right
Q2. In a series, a dot moves round the corners of a square — top-left, top-right, bottom-right — and the number of short lines inside goes 1, 2, 3. What is figure 4?
Two parts, two rules. The dot moves one corner clockwise each step, so it goes to the bottom-left. The lines increase by one, so there are 4. Answer: dot at the bottom-left corner, with 4 lines
Q3. Three figures are a triangle with 3 dots, a square with 4 dots and a pentagon with 5 dots. The fourth is a hexagon with 4 dots. Which is the odd one?
In the first three the dots equal the number of sides. The hexagon has 6 sides but only 4 dots. Answer: the hexagon
Q4. A square with a small circle inside : a circle with a small square inside :: a triangle with a small circle inside : ?
The change is that the inner and outer shapes swap. Applying it to the third figure puts the circle outside and the triangle inside. Answer: a circle with a small triangle inside
Q5. A triangle has 3 lines drawn from its top vertex to the base. How many triangles are there in the figure?
With n lines from the vertex the base is split into n + 1 = 4 parts, and every pair of the 5 lines from the vertex makes a triangle: 4 × 5 / 2 = 10. Answer: 10
Q6. How many squares are there in a 4 × 4 grid of small squares?
Count by size: 1 × 1 squares 16, 2 × 2 squares 9, 3 × 3 squares 4, and the whole grid 1. 16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 30. Answer: 30
Q7. How many rectangles (squares included) are there in a grid of 2 rows and 3 columns?
A rectangle is fixed by choosing 2 of the 3 horizontal lines and 2 of the 4 vertical lines: 3 × 6 = 18. Answer: 18

How to Prepare Figure Series, Classification, Analogy & Counting for SSC CGL

  1. Follow one part at a time. In a series, track each shape, dot or line separately across all the figures and write its rule.
  2. Know the turning steps. 45°, 90° and 135°, clockwise or anticlockwise, cover most series; check whether the turn grows each step.
  3. In classification, name the shared property. Turned copies, dots matching sides, or matching inner and outer shapes — then find the one that breaks it.
  4. In analogy, list every change before looking at the options. If figure 1 → 2 has two changes, the answer must show both.
  5. Count in groups. Small pieces first, then shapes made of two, three and four pieces; use n(n+1)/2 and the grid formulas where they apply.
  6. Do Level 1 untimed first, then with the 15-minute clock. Move to Level 2 once Level 1 is steady, and then check yourself on a previous year paper.

SSC CGL Figure Series, Classification, Analogy & Counting — Frequently Asked Questions

How many figure series, classification, analogy and counting questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one to three questions from this group in a Tier I shift. Figure series and counting of triangles are the most regular; figure classification and figure analogy appear in most shifts as well. The same forms appear in SSC CHSL, MTS and CGL Tier II.
How do I solve a figure series question?
Follow one part of the figure at a time across the series: a shape that turns, a dot that moves, a line that is added or a shading that changes. Write the rule for each part, such as 45 degrees clockwise each step, then apply every rule once more to get the next figure, and check the options part by part.
How do I find the odd figure in a classification question?
Look for the property three figures share: the same shape turned, a number of dots equal to the number of sides, or an inner shape matching the outer one. The figure that breaks it is the answer. A mirror image among three turned copies is the most common odd one.
How do I solve a figure analogy question?
Find every change from figure 1 to figure 2: a turn, a mirror image, shading reversed, inner and outer shapes swapping, or a side added. Apply exactly the same changes to figure 3. In harder questions there are two changes at once, and a wrong option usually makes only one of them.
What is the formula for counting triangles in a figure?
If n lines are drawn from the top vertex of a triangle to its base, the triangles number (n + 1)(n + 2) / 2. A square with both diagonals has 8 triangles. For bigger figures, count in groups: triangles made of one small piece, then two pieces, then four, and add the groups.
How do I count squares and rectangles in a grid?
In an n by n grid the squares are 1² + 2² + … + n², so a 4 by 4 grid has 30. In a grid of m rows and n columns the rectangles, squares included, are C(m+1, 2) × C(n+1, 2), so a 2 by 3 grid has 3 × 6 = 18.

What is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2?
Level 1 is set at real SSC CGL difficulty: series of four figures with one or two changes, analogies with one change, and counting figures of moderate size. Level 2 is harder than the exam: series of five figures with two or three parts changing at once, turns that grow each step, three-step shading cycles, analogies with two changes at once, and larger counting figures.
