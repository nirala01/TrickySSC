Simple interest is the friendliest chapter in commercial arithmetic: one formula, four letters, and every year earning exactly the same as every other. That last point is the whole idea. The interest is always charged on the original sum, never on the interest already earned, which is what separates it from compound interest and what makes every year’s earning identical.

The marks are lost almost entirely on two things: a time that was never converted into years, and the difference between the interest and the amount. A period of 8 months is two thirds of a year, not 8; a sum that becomes three times itself has earned interest equal to twice the sum, not three times. Each test puts twenty-five simple interest questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes the recurring shapes visible — the same doubling questions, the same equal-interest splits, the same installment setups SSC has been recycling for years.

What You Will Practice in the Simple Interest Chapter

Core topics

  • The formula in all four directions — finding the interest, the principal, the rate or the time
  • The amount, and working back from an amount to the sum that produced it
  • Time given in months, in days, as a mixed fraction of a year, or between two dates
  • Rates quoted per month, per quarter or per half-year, and paise per rupee per month
  • Sums that double, treble or reach any stated multiple, and the time or rate that does it
  • The interest as a fraction of the sum, or of the amount — and the difference between the two
  • Two sums earning equal interest, and the ratio that condition forces
  • A sum split between two rates, with the total interest, the split or one rate unknown
  • Rates that change part way through the term, in two or three slabs
  • Rates that step up or down by a fixed amount each year
  • Interchanging the two parts of a split, and what that does to the interest
  • Part repayment of a loan, and the settlement payment that clears it
  • Equal annual installments, paid at the start or the end of each year
  • Hire purchase — a down payment, a deferred balance, and the cash price
  • Deposits made every year, half-year or month, and the maturity value
  • Date-to-date interest, counting days and handling a leap year

Formulas worth memorising

SituationFormula
Simple interestSI = P × R × T / 100
AmountA = P + SI = P(100 + RT) / 100
Principal from the amountP = A × 100 / (100 + RT)
Rate, or time, from the interestR = SI × 100 / (P × T)
Time in months or daysT = months / 12 or days / 365
Sum becomes n times itselfR = (n − 1) × 100 / T
Interest as a share of the sumSI / P = RT / 100
Two sums earning equal interestP₁R₁T₁ = P₂R₂T₂
Two amounts at two datesd = (A₂ − A₁) / (T₂ − T₁), then P = A₁ − dT₁
Interchanging the parts of a splitchange = (P₁ − P₂)(R₁ − R₂)T / 100
Debt cleared by n year-end installmentsD = nx + (xR / 100) × [(n−1) + … + 0]

The multiple row is the one to hold on to: to become n times itself, a sum must earn interest equal to (n − 1) times itself, because the amount already contains the sum once over. That single correction settles a large share of the chapter’s questions.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Leaving the time in months or days. The rate is per annum, so the time must be in years before anything is multiplied. Eight months is 8/12 of a year; seventy-three days is 73/365. Using 8 or 73 makes the answer twelve or three hundred times too large.
  • Reading a multiple as the interest. A sum that becomes 4 times itself has earned 3 times itself in interest, not 4. The sum was there from the start and is counted once inside the amount.
  • Working a rate on the amount instead of the principal. The amount already carries the interest inside it. Dividing by the amount gives a smaller rate that answers a different question.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Simple Interest

Q1. Find the simple interest on ₹7,200 at 9% per annum for 8 months.
Turn the period into years first: 8 months is 8/12 = 2/3 of a year. One year’s interest is 7200 × 9 / 100 = 648, so two thirds of that is 432. Using 8 in place of 2/3 would give ₹5,184 — twelve times too much. Answer: ₹432
Q2. A sum becomes 3 times itself in 16 years at simple interest. What is the rate per cent per annum?
Trebling means the interest alone is twice the sum, not three times — the sum is already inside the amount. So R × 16 / 100 = 2, giving R = 200/16 = 12.5. Answer: 12.5% per annum
Q3. A sum amounts to ₹6,300 in 3 years and to ₹6,800 in 5 years at simple interest. Find the sum.
The gap between two amounts is pure interest, so two years are worth 6800 − 6300 = 500 and one year is 250. By the third year the sum had earned 250 × 3 = 750, so P = 6300 − 750. Note that neither the rate nor the sum was given — the two readings carry both. Answer: ₹5,550
Q4. ₹10,000 is divided into two parts, lent at 6% and 10% per annum, and the total interest for one year is ₹760. Find the part lent at 10%.
Suppose all of it sat at 6%: that would bring 10000 × 6 / 100 = 600. The real figure is ₹760, so ₹160 extra comes from the money at 10%. Every rupee moved from 6% to 10% earns 4 more per hundred, that is ₹0.04 a year, so the part at 10% is 160 / 0.04. Answer: ₹4,000

How to Prepare Simple Interest for SSC CGL

  1. Do percentage first. Every rate in this chapter is a percentage, and a student who is fluent with percentages usually finds simple interest takes an afternoon.
  2. Convert the time before you write anything else. Months over 12, days over 365, and a rate quoted per month or per quarter multiplied up to a year. Most wrong answers in this chapter are correct arithmetic on an unconverted period.
  3. Learn to work in shares of the sum. Writing SI/P = RT/100 lets you answer any question that gives a fraction or a ratio instead of rupees — and a good third of the chapter does exactly that.
  4. Fix the multiple rule in your head. To become n times, the interest must be (n − 1) times the sum. Doubling needs interest equal to the sum, trebling twice the sum, and so on. It takes ten seconds and it recurs constantly.
  5. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right form of the formula, not speed.
  6. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. A Tier I simple interest question that takes more than about forty seconds usually means the formula was rearranged the long way round.
  7. Then do compound interest. The two chapters use the same letters and are regularly compared in the same question, so they belong together.
  8. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 1 is consistent under the clock, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Simple Interest — Frequently Asked Questions

How many simple interest questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one to two questions in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and two to three in Tier II Paper I. Counted with compound interest, which shares its formulas, the pair is worth three to five marks in most shifts.
What topics come under simple interest for SSC CGL?
The basic formula in all four directions, the amount and working back to the principal, time given in months, days or a mixed fraction, rates quoted per month or per quarter, sums that double or treble, two sums earning equal interest, a sum split between two rates, rates that change part way through the term, part repayment of a loan, equal annual installments, hire purchase and the cash price, and date-to-date interest including leap years.
What is the formula for simple interest?
SI = P × R × T / 100, where P is the principal, R the rate per cent per annum and T the time in years. The amount is A = P + SI. The time must always be converted into years before the formula is used — months are divided by 12 and days by 365.

If a sum doubles in 8 years, what is the rate of simple interest?
12.5% per annum. Doubling means the interest alone equals the principal, so R × 8 / 100 = 1 and R = 100/8. The same reasoning gives the rate for any multiple: to become n times, the interest must be (n − 1) times the sum, so R = (n − 1) × 100 / T.
Which chapters should I study along with simple interest?
Percentage first, because every rate in the chapter is a percentage. Then compound interest, which uses the same principal, rate and time but lets the interest earn in its turn — the two are almost always taught and tested as a pair. Profit and loss and discount use the same commercial vocabulary and are worth doing in the same block.
