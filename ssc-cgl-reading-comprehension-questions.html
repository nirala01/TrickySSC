Every comprehension question asks one thing: what does this passage actually say? Not what is true in the world, not what sounds sensible, but what the passage states or clearly supports. The right option can always be traced back to a line in the text; the wrong ones go beyond it, twist it, or contradict it.

Marks are lost in three places. The first is answering from general knowledge — an option can be perfectly true and still be wrong because the passage never says it. The second is the extreme word — always, never, only and entirely turn a fair statement into a wrong one. The third is the dictionary meaning: in vocabulary questions, one option is usually another real meaning of the word, just not the one used in this sentence.

What You Will Practice in the Reading Comprehension Chapter

Questions on the whole passage

  • The central idea, main theme or most suitable title
  • The author’s tone or attitude: objective, critical, appreciative, concerned, sarcastic
  • The author’s purpose: to inform, to warn, to argue, to describe

Questions on one part of it

  • Facts stated in the passage: “According to the passage, why…”
  • “Which of the following is NOT true / NOT mentioned?”
  • Inference: what the passage suggests or implies without saying it in the same words
  • Vocabulary in context: the synonym or antonym of a word as it is used in the passage

The six question types — and where the answer is

Question typeWhere to look
Central idea / titleThe first and last paragraphs together — the idea that links them, not a single example
Fact (“according to the passage”)One sentence that states it — find the key word from the question in the text
NOT true / NOT mentionedCheck each option against the text; three are there, one is missing or contradicted
InferenceTwo sentences read together, or what the author’s argument clearly leads to
Vocabulary in contextThe sentence the word is in — which meaning fits there
Tone / purposeThe author’s word choices, especially adjectives and the closing lines

Every solution in these tests quotes the exact line that decides the answer, so you learn where to look, not just what to pick.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Picking what is true, not what is stated. If the passage does not say it or clearly support it, it is wrong — however true it is in real life.
  • Taking an example for the main idea. A vivid story or a striking detail is rarely the central idea; the title has to cover the whole passage.
  • Answering vocabulary questions from the dictionary. Read the sentence first, decide the meaning there, and only then look at the options.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Reading Comprehension

Read the passage, then the four questions below it.

In a small town, a group of neighbours noticed that most households owned a drill, a ladder and a garden spade that were used only a few times a year. So they opened a tool library in an unused room of the community hall. Members pay a small yearly fee and borrow tools the way readers borrow books. The organisers say the savings are welcome, but the bigger change is social: people who once only nodded at each other in the street now stop to share advice on fixing a tap or pruning a tree. A few residents still prefer to own their tools, worried that an item will not be free when they need it.
Q1. Which of the following best states the central idea of the passage?
(a) Most families cannot afford to buy their own tools
(b) A shared tool library saves money and brings neighbours closer
(c) Residents refuse to share their tools with one another
(d) Community halls are being used only as libraries
The passage begins with the reason for the library and ends with its effects — savings and a friendlier street. (a) and (d) are never said, and (c) is true of only “a few residents”. Answer: (b)
Q2. According to the passage, how do members use the tool library?
(a) They buy tools from it at a lower price
(b) They hire tools by the hour from the organisers
(c) They pay a yearly fee and borrow tools like books
(d) They donate one tool every month to the hall
The passage states it directly: members “pay a small yearly fee and borrow tools the way readers borrow books”. Buying, hourly hire and monthly donations are not mentioned. Answer: (c)
Q3. It can be inferred that before the library opened, the neighbours
(a) rarely talked to each other beyond a greeting
(b) often lent their tools to one another
(c) quarrelled over shared garden space
(d) did not own any tools of their own
They “once only nodded at each other” and now stop to talk, so earlier they hardly spoke. (b) and (c) have no support, and (d) is contradicted by the first sentence. Answer: (a)
Q4. Which word is most similar in meaning to ‘welcome’ as used in the passage?
(a) greeting  (b) invited  (c) famous  (d) pleasing
“The savings are welcome” means the savings are pleasing and gladly received. “Greeting” and “invited” are other meanings of the word that do not fit this sentence — the classic trap. Answer: (d) pleasing

How to Prepare Reading Comprehension for SSC CGL

  1. Glance at the questions first. Ten seconds tells you which facts and words to watch for while you read.
  2. Read the whole passage once, at a steady pace. Notice the topic, the turn in the argument (“however”, “yet”) and where the author’s own view appears.
  3. Go back to the line for every fact. Do not answer from memory; find the sentence and compare it with the option.
  4. Strike out the extreme options. Always, never, only, all and entirely are wrong far more often than they are right.
  5. Answer the central idea and tone last. By then you have read the passage closely enough to see what it is really about.
  6. Do Level 1 untimed first, then with the 15-minute clock. Move to Level 2 once Level 1 is steady, and then check yourself on a previous year paper.

SSC CGL Reading Comprehension — Frequently Asked Questions

How many reading comprehension questions are asked in SSC CGL?
In SSC CGL and CHSL Tier I, a reading comprehension passage with five questions appears in some shifts, often in place of the cloze test. In the Tier II English paper, comprehension is a regular part of the section, with several passages.
What kinds of questions are asked on an RC passage?
Six kinds come up again and again: the central idea or best title, a fact stated in the passage, which statement is NOT true, an inference the passage supports, the meaning of a word as used in the passage, and the author’s tone or purpose.
Should I read the questions before the passage?
A quick look at the questions first helps, because you then know which facts to watch for. After that, read the whole passage once at a steady pace, and go back to the exact line for each fact or vocabulary question instead of answering from memory.
Are the passages in these tests taken from previous year papers?
No. All 100 passages are written fresh for TrickySSC in the SSC pattern, on new topics, and none repeats or adapts a previous year comprehension passage, so every test is unseen practice.
What is the most common trap in RC questions?
An option that is true in real life but not stated in the passage. The next most common are options with extreme words such as always, never and only, options that take one detail as the main idea, and the other dictionary meaning of a vocabulary word.
How many questions are in each test?
Twenty-five questions per test, from five passages of five questions each, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the tests in English or Hindi?
The reading comprehension tests are in English, as the English Language section is in SSC CGL and CHSL. Every solution quotes the line from the passage that decides the answer and explains why each wrong option fails, in simple English.
What is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2?
Level 1 is set at real SSC CGL difficulty, with 220 to 310 word passages on everyday topics and answers found in one clear line. Level 2 is harder than Tier I: 330 to 450 word argumentative passages, more inference questions, harder vocabulary and wrong options that are only partly wrong.
