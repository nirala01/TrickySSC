Index results that skip the working

  • Equal index sums: if p + q = r + s then ap + aq = ar + as; terms equidistant from the ends always add to a + l.
  • Middle term: for an odd count Sn = n × middle term; for an even count Sn = n/2 × the middle pair.
  • Swapped index and value: ap = q with aq = p gives ap+q = 0; and Sm = Sn gives Sm+n = 0.
  • Sums to terms: put n = 2m − 1 into a ratio of sums to get the ratio of the mth terms.
  • GP products: p + q = u + v gives tptq = tutv; the product of an odd number of GP terms is the middle term to that power.

Also worth memorising

  • Sum of the first n odd numbers = n2; of the first n even numbers = n(n + 1)
  • Sn = An2 + Bn is always an AP with d = 2A and first term A + B
  • k arithmetic means: d = (l − a)/(k + 1), and the mth mean is a + md — not a + (m − 1)d
  • k geometric means: rk+1 = l/a, and the mth mean is a rm
  • AM ≥ GM ≥ HM, with GM2 = AM × HM, and (x − y)2 = 4(AM2 − GM2)
  • Nested GP sums: S2n/Sn = 1 + rn. Side-by-side blocks: next n / first n = rn
  • Σn2 to 10 = 385 and to 20 = 2870; Σn3 to 12 = 6084

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Number Series (AP & GP)

Q1. Find the 21st term of the arithmetic progression 7, 12, 17, 22, …
a = 7 and d = 5, so a21 = 7 + (21 − 1)×5 = 7 + 100. The multiplier is 20, not 21 — the first term needs no step. Answer: 107
Q2. Find the sum of all multiples of 7 lying between 100 and 500.
First 105, last 497, so n = (497 − 105)/7 + 1 = 57 and S = 57/2 × (105 + 497) = 57 × 301. "Between" means strictly inside, so 98 and 504 are out. Answer: 17157
Q3. Three numbers in AP have sum 27 and product 585. Find the largest.
Take them as a − d, a, a + d. The sum gives 3a = 27, so a = 9, and 9(81 − d2) = 585 gives d2 = 16. The numbers are 5, 9, 13. Answer: 13
Q4. An arithmetic progression of 25 terms has sum 800. Find its 13th term.
With an odd count, Sn = n × middle term, and the middle of 25 terms is the 13th. So a13 = 800/25 — no common difference is needed, and none can be found. Answer: 32
Q5. The sum to infinity of a GP is 15 and the sum to infinity of the squares of its terms is 45. Find its first term.
a/(1 − r) = 15 gives a = 15(1 − r). Squaring makes a GP with first term a2 and ratio r2, so a2/(1 − r2) = 45. Cancelling (1 − r) leaves 225(1 − r)/(1 + r) = 45, so r = 2/3 and a = 5. Answer: 5

How to Prepare Series (AP & GP) for SSC CGL

  1. Learn the four core formulas before anything else. The nth term and sum of an AP, and the nth term and sum of a GP, carry most of the chapter on their own.
  2. Say "one step fewer" every time. Both term formulas carry n − 1, and getting that wrong shifts the answer by exactly one term — which is always among the options.
  3. Use the symmetric forms whenever three or four numbers are described. a − d, a, a + d for an AP and a/r, a, ar for a GP turn a two-unknown problem into a one-line one.
  4. Learn the index results as a family. Equal index sums, the middle term and Sm = Sn solve the questions where a and d cannot be found at all.
  5. Check |r| < 1 before writing an infinite sum. If the ratio is 1 or more in size, the sum does not exist and the question is asking something else.
  6. Test every root against the words of the question. A quadratic in n often has two roots, and "positive", "increasing" or a row that cannot hold negative trees is what picks between them.
  7. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the formulas. The aim is correct recall, not speed.
  8. Move to Level 2 with the 30-minute clock on. A term or sum that takes more than twenty seconds means you are still deriving the formula instead of applying it.
  9. Study it with the standard sums. Do number system before, and keep Σn2 and Σn3 at hand for simplification.
  10. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 2 is consistent, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Series (AP & GP) — Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an AP and a GP?
In an arithmetic progression each term is the previous one plus a fixed number, the common difference. In a geometric progression each term is the previous one times a fixed number, the common ratio. Subtract consecutive terms to test for an AP; if the differences are not constant, divide instead and check the ratios.
What are the main AP and GP formulas for SSC CGL?
an = a + (n − 1)d and Sn = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d] = n/2 (a + l) for an AP; tn = a rn−1, Sn = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) and S∞ = a/(1 − r) for a GP. Add Σn = n(n+1)/2, Σn2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 and Σn3 = [n(n+1)/2]2.
When does a geometric progression have a sum to infinity?
Only when |r| < 1, that is when the size of the ratio, ignoring its sign, is below 1. Then the terms shrink towards zero and the running total settles on a/(1 − r). If |r| is 1 or more, no infinite sum exists.
How do I solve questions where three numbers are in AP or GP?
Use symmetric symbols. Three in AP are a − d, a, a + d, so the sum is 3a and a comes out at once. Three in GP are a/r, a, ar, so the product is a3 and a is its cube root. The second condition then gives d or r.
How many series, AP and GP questions are asked in SSC CGL?
One to two in a typical Tier I shift — usually an nth term, a sum, or the total of the multiples of a number inside a range. The chapter also feeds the standard sums of squares and cubes used in simplification, so its formulas are worth more than the direct count.
What is a harmonic progression and how is it solved?
A harmonic progression is a list whose reciprocals form an AP — 6, 4, 3, … is one because 1/6, 1/4, 1/3 rise in equal steps. There is no direct HP formula: invert every term, solve inside the AP of reciprocals, and invert the answer at the end.
