A race question is a speed question with the clock stopped at the winner’s finish. Whatever the wording — a start, a beat, a dead heat, a lead — it comes down to one sentence: in the time the winner runs the whole course, the loser runs less, and the two distances are in the ratio of the speeds. Everything else is reading which distance and which time the question is really about.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is adding margins — if A beats B by 20 m and B beats C by 10 m over 200 m, A does not beat C by 30 m; C’s shortfall is measured when B has run 180 m, not 200 m, so the answer is 29 m. The second is mixing the two kinds of start: a start of 10 seconds is worth 10 × (the slower runner’s speed) metres, never 10 × the winner’s. The third is on circular tracks — using the sum of speeds for runners going the same way, or the LCM rule for a meeting anywhere when it applies only to a meeting at the starting point. Each test puts twenty-five race questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those checks automatic.

What You Will Practice in the Race Chapter

Linear (straight) races

  • Both finishing times given, and the distance by which the faster runner wins
  • A head start in seconds, both finishing together — the winner’s time or speed, or the loser’s
  • A head start in metres for a dead heat — the other runner’s speed, or the start that can be given
  • A speed ratio and a lead, and the point on the course where the runners meet
  • A speed ratio and a start turned into a winning margin, and the start recovered from a margin
  • A start plus a time margin turned into the loser’s or the winner’s speed
  • A margin from one race carried to a longer race, with or without a fresh start
  • Two races with different starts solved together — a time, a speed, or both speeds
  • A beats B and B beats C over the same course, chained into A against C
  • Margins over different course lengths, or one winner’s two margins, chained
  • Pursuit with a head start in metres and in seconds at once
  • A second race in which the losers are handed starts
  • “Beats by d metres or t seconds” — the loser’s speed, the winner’s time, or the race length

Circular races

  • First meeting in the same direction (difference of speeds) and in opposite directions (sum of speeds)
  • Both directions in one question, and the ratio of the two meeting times
  • First time back at the starting point together — the LCM of the lap times
  • Three runners in mixed directions, and the first moment all three are together
  • Speeds in rounds per hour and a clock start
  • A fixed race distance on a track — the last overtake and the number of overtakes
  • The number of distinct points on the track where the runners meet
  • A speed multiple and a meeting time turned into a lap time
  • Speeds exchanged, doubled or halved at a meeting; a runner who turns back
  • Overtaking a runner who starts with a lead, and a runner who starts late
  • The nth meeting as a clock time, laps completed at a meeting, and where on the track it happens

The first row carries the linear half of the chapter and the eighth and ninth carry the circular half. Almost every other rule is one of those three read with the right distance.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Adding margins. A beats B by 20 m and B beats C by 10 m over 200 m gives 29 m, not 30 m — B’s lead over C is earned over 200 m, but B only ran 180 m by the time A finished. Chain the ratios instead.
  • Valuing a time start at the wrong speed. A start of 10 seconds is the slower runner’s 10 seconds. Multiply by the loser’s speed, and convert every start to one kind before comparing two races.
  • Same direction, wrong rule. Runners going the same way need the difference of speeds; the sum is for opposite directions. And the LCM of lap times gives the first meeting at the starting point, not the first meeting anywhere.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Race Questions

Q1. In a 1 km race, A beats B by 40 metres or 8 seconds. What is B’s speed?
The two readings of the margin describe the same gap: when A finishes, B is 40 m short and needs 8 s more. So B covers 40 m in 8 s: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 m/s. The race length is not needed for B’s speed at all — it is only needed for A’s time (1000 ÷ 5 − 8 = 192 s). Answer: 5 m/s
Q2. In a 200 m race, P beats Q by 20 m and Q beats R by 10 m. By how much does P beat R?
When P runs 200 m, Q runs 180 m. R runs 190 for every 200 of Q, so when Q has run 180 m, R has run 180 × 190 ÷ 200 = 171 m. P beats R by 200 − 171 = 29 m. Adding 20 + 10 = 30 m is the trap. Answer: 29 m
Q3. In a 600 m race the speeds of A and B are in the ratio 5 : 3. A gives B a start of 200 m. By how much does A win?
In the time A runs 600 m, B runs 600 × 3/5 = 360 m. B needs only 600 − 200 = 400 m, so he is 400 − 360 = 40 m short. Shortcut: the fair start is 600 − 360 = 240 m; B got 200 m, so A wins by 40 m. Answer: 40 m
Q4. Two runners start together from a point on a 1200 m circular track at 10 m/s and 6 m/s. After how long do they first meet running in opposite directions, and running in the same direction?
Opposite directions: they close the whole track together at 10 + 6 = 16 m/s, so 1200 ÷ 16 = 75 s. Same direction: the faster must gain a full lap at 10 − 6 = 4 m/s, so 1200 ÷ 4 = 300 s. The same-direction time is always the longer one. Answer: 75 s and 300 s

How to Prepare Races for SSC CGL

  1. Stop the clock at the winner’s finish. Ask how far each runner has gone at that instant; every linear race question is answered from that picture.
  2. Turn every margin into a speed ratio first. “A beats B by 40 m over 400 m” means speeds 10 : 9. Ratios carry across race lengths; margins do not.
  3. Convert starts to one kind. A start in seconds is worth (seconds × slower speed) metres. Do the conversion before comparing two races.
  4. Chain, never add. For three runners multiply the distance ratios; adding the two margins is the most common wrong option.
  5. Decide who wins before finding the margin. If the start given exceeds the fair start, the slower runner wins, and the margin is measured on the other runner at that finish.
  6. On a track, name the direction first. Same way: difference of speeds. Opposite: sum. Write it down before dividing.
  7. Know which meeting is asked. Anywhere on the track uses relative speed; at the starting point uses the LCM of lap times; the number of distinct points uses the HCF.
  8. Keep a lead and a lap apart. The first overtake closes only the lead; every later overtake needs a whole lap.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right rule, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Race Questions — Frequently Asked Questions

How many race questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually none or one in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and none or one in Tier II Paper I. When it appears it is a single rule applied once — a start, a beat, or a circular-track meeting — and it should take well under a minute.
What topics come under races for SSC CGL?
Linear races: winning margins from finishing times, head starts in metres or seconds, dead heats, speed ratios with a lead, "beats by d metres or t seconds", margins carried to other race lengths, chained beats between three runners and two-race puzzles. Circular races: first meeting in the same or opposite directions, meeting at the starting point by LCM, three runners, rounds per hour, distinct meeting points, laps at a meeting, and overtaking with a lead or a late start.
What does "A beats B by 40 metres or 8 seconds" mean?
Both describe the same gap at A's finish: B is 40 m behind and needs 8 more seconds to cover it. So B's speed is 40 ÷ 8 = 5 m/s. From that, B's full time is the race length divided by 5, and A's time is 8 seconds less.
How do I chain "A beats B by a and B beats C by b"?
Multiply the distance ratios, never add the margins. Over a course L, when A runs L, B runs L − a; when B runs L, C runs L − b; so when A runs L, C runs (L − a)(L − b) ÷ L. Over 200 m with a = 20 and b = 10 that is 180 × 190 ÷ 200 = 171 m, so A beats C by 29 m, not 30.
What is a dead heat?
A finish in which both runners reach the line at the same instant. If A gives B a start of d metres and it ends in a dead heat, the speeds are in the ratio L : (L − d). The start that produces a dead heat is called the fair start; a smaller start means the faster runner still wins by the difference.
When do runners on a circular track meet?
Running the same way they meet every L ÷ (difference of speeds) seconds, because the faster runner must gain a full lap. Running opposite ways they meet every L ÷ (sum of speeds) seconds. They are both back at the starting point together after the LCM of their lap times, and the number of distinct points where they meet is the sum or difference of the speeds divided by their HCF.
How many questions are in each race practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the race tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with races?
Time, speed and distance first, because a race is a relative-speed question with the clock stopped at the winner's finish. Ratio and proportion covers the speed ratios, LCM and HCF cover the circular-track meeting rules, and quadratic equations help with the Level 2 questions where the speeds differ by a fixed amount.
