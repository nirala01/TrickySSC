What You Will Practice in the Quadratic Equation Chapter

Sum and product of the roots — the two numbers that answer everything

For ax² + bx + c = 0 with roots α and β, the sum is −b/a and the product is c/a. Every symmetric expression in the roots is reachable from those two alone, which is why the roots themselves are almost never worth finding.

Two of these are worth memorising as sentences rather than formulas. The gap between the roots squared IS the discriminant divided by a², so any question about how far apart the roots are is a discriminant question in disguise. And reciprocal roots mean the product is 1, which happens exactly when c = a.

The discriminant and the nature of the roots

D = b² − 4ac decides everything about what kind of roots exist, before any of them is found.

  • D > 0 — two distinct real roots. If D is also a perfect square, they are rational and the expression factorises over whole numbers.
  • D = 0 — the two roots merge into one repeated root, and the expression is a perfect square such as (2x − 3)². A zero discriminant is easy to misread as “no roots”; it means the opposite.
  • D < 0 — no real roots. The sum and product relations still hold and still give real answers, which is why a question can ask for α² + β² even here.

When a parameter sits inside the coefficients, the equal-roots condition D = 0 becomes an equation in that parameter. If the parameter appears squared you get two values and both must be reported; if the squared terms cancel you get one. Read which case you are in before assuming the answer count.

Signs and position of the roots

The product fixes the signs and the sum separates the remaining cases. A negative product means one root of each sign, and nothing else needs checking — a negative product also guarantees the roots are real. A positive product with a positive sum means both are positive; a positive product with a negative sum means both are negative. In each of those two cases the discriminant must still be tested separately, and it frequently trims the range a careless answer would allow.

For questions about where the roots sit relative to a number t, use the curve. With a positive leading coefficient the expression is negative strictly between its roots, so f(t) < 0 means t lies between them. To place both roots beyond t you need three conditions at once: D ≥ 0, f(t) > 0, and the turning point −b/2a on the correct side of t.

Building the equation whose roots are transformed

Find the new sum and the new product from the old ones, then write x² − (new sum)x + (new product) = 0 and clear any fractions at the end. The original roots are never needed.

The reciprocal row has a shortcut worth knowing: reversing the coefficients of the original equation produces the reciprocal-root equation directly, so 2x² − 7x + 3 = 0 becomes 3x² − 7x + 2 = 0. Note also that the shifted product is not P + h² — the cross term hS is the piece most often dropped.

Common roots

If two quadratics share ONE root, subtract them. The shared root satisfies both and therefore satisfies the difference, and the x² terms cancel to leave a linear equation that yields the root at once. Scale one equation first if the leading coefficients differ. The subtraction gives a candidate only, so substitute it back to find the unknown.

If they share BOTH roots, the two equations describe the same pair of numbers and can differ only by an overall scaling, so their coefficients are in proportion: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂. Reading which of the two situations the question describes is half the work.

Factor theorem, remainder theorem and equations in disguise

If (x − p) is a factor of a polynomial then the polynomial is zero at x = p; a stated remainder means it equals that remainder instead. The same substitution answers both. When the divisor is (mx + n) the test value is the fraction −n/m, and when the divisor is a quadratic the remainder is of the form rx + s, which needs two substitutions to pin down.

A large family of questions is quadratic only after one move: a fractional equation cleared of denominators, a radical equation squared, a quartic in x², a repeated block replaced by a single letter, or an equation divided through by x to reach x + 1/x. In every one of these the last step is the one candidates forget — converting back, and discarding any value that breaks the original equation.

Consistency of linear equations in two variables

Compare the three ratios of a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂:

  • a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ — the lines cross once, so there is exactly one solution whatever the constants are.
  • a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ — the same line twice, so infinitely many solutions.
  • a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ — parallel and distinct, so no solution at all.

The constants are what separate the last two cases, so the third ratio is never a formality. When a parameter appears in several places, solve with two ratios and then TEST the answer in the third — a candidate that passes the first pair can still fail there.

Maximum and minimum of a quadratic expression

Complete the square: x² + bx + c = (x + b/2)² + (c − b²/4). A square is never negative, so with a positive leading coefficient the leftover constant is the minimum, reached at x = −b/2a; with a negative leading coefficient it is the maximum. Factor the leading coefficient out before completing the square, and read the question carefully — the position x and the value are different answers and both are offered.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Quadratic Equation

Example 1 — a symmetric function without finding the roots. If α and β are the roots of x² − 7x + 12 = 0, find α² + β².

Read the two numbers off the coefficients: S = 7 and P = 12. Then α² + β² = S² − 2P = 49 − 24 = 25. The roots happen to be 3 and 4 here, but finding them was never necessary — and with roots like (5 ± √13)/2 it would have cost you the question.

Example 2 — equal roots giving two answers. For which values of k does x² + (k − 3)x + 9 = 0 have equal roots?

Equal roots means D = 0: (k − 3)² − 36 = 0, so (k − 3)² = 36 and k − 3 = ±6. That gives k = 9 or −3. Both are genuine — at k = 9 the equation is (x + 3)² and at k = −3 it is (x − 3)². Taking only the positive square root loses half the answer.

Example 3 — a common root. x² − 9x + k = 0 and x² − 11x + 3k = 0 share a root. Find the non-zero value of k.

Subtract: 2x − 2k = 0, so the shared root is x = k itself. Substituting into the first equation gives k² − 9k + k = 0, that is k(k − 8) = 0, so k = 8. Check it: x² − 9x + 8 = (x − 1)(x − 8) and x² − 11x + 24 = (x − 3)(x − 8), sharing the root 8.

Example 4 — transformed roots. α and β are the roots of 2x² − 7x + 3 = 0. Find the equation whose roots are 1/α and 1/β.

Here S = 7/2 and P = 3/2. New sum = S/P = 7/3; new product = 1/P = 2/3. So x² − (7/3)x + 2/3 = 0, and multiplying through by 3 gives 3x² − 7x + 2 = 0. Notice the coefficients have simply reversed — that shortcut works for reciprocal roots every time.

Example 5 — consistency of a pair of lines. For what value of k do 3x + 5y = 8 and 9x + ky = 24 have infinitely many solutions?

Infinitely many solutions needs all three ratios equal. The x-terms give 3/9 = 1/3 and the constants give 8/24 = 1/3, so the y-terms must match: 5/k = 1/3, giving k = 15. Had the constants been 8 and 25, that same k would have produced parallel lines and NO solution — the third ratio is what separates the two cases.

How to Prepare Quadratic Equation for SSC CGL

  1. Write the sum and the product first, every single time. Before reading the rest of the question, put S = −b/a and P = c/a on the page. A large share of this chapter is answered from those two lines alone.
  2. Stop solving for the roots. If the question is symmetric in α and β, the roots are a detour. Finding them is the single biggest time loss in the chapter.
  3. Learn the four identities cold — S² − 2P, S² − 4P, S³ − 3PS and S/P. Everything else in the chapter is built by combining them.
  4. Check where the parameter sits before counting answers. A parameter in the constant term usually gives one value; a parameter that ends up squared usually gives two. Reporting one when there are two is a common half-mark loss.
  5. Treat the discriminant as a range tool, not just a yes/no test. Questions asking for which values of k the roots are real, distinct, equal or of a given sign are discriminant questions with an inequality at the end.
  6. For common roots, subtract; for both roots common, use proportion. Two different situations, two different methods, and the question always tells you which.
  7. Undo every substitution. Whether you set y = x², t = x + 1/x or named a repeated block, the answer asked for is in x. Handing back the helper variable is the most frequent error in the disguised-equation family.
  8. Do Level 1 untimed first. The aim is the correct relation, not speed. Move to Level 2 with the 15-minute clock on — that is 36 seconds a question, the real Tier I pace.
  9. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 2 is consistent, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Quadratic Equation — Frequently Asked Questions

How many quadratic equation questions are asked in SSC CGL?
One or two in a typical Tier I shift and two to four in Tier II. The chapter also feeds several others: the sum-and-product relations reappear in algebraic identities, the discriminant decides consistency questions, and the same factorising work runs through simplification. Counted with its dependants it is worth far more than its own two marks.
Do I have to solve a quadratic to answer questions about its roots?
Usually not, and that is the whole skill of the chapter. For ax² + bx + c = 0 the roots add to −b/a and multiply to c/a. Anything symmetric in the two roots — their squares, their cubes, their reciprocals, the gap between them — can be built from those two numbers alone, without ever finding a root.
What does the discriminant tell me?
D = b² − 4ac settles the nature of the roots. If D > 0 there are two distinct real roots; if D = 0 the two roots merge into one repeated root and the expression is a perfect square; if D < 0 there are no real roots at all. If D is also a perfect square the roots are rational.
How do I find the value of k that makes the roots equal?
Set b² − 4ac = 0 with k substituted wherever it appears, then solve the equation that results. Watch where k sits: if it appears only in the constant you get one value, but if it appears squared — inside the middle coefficient, for instance — you get a quadratic in k and usually two answers, both of which must be reported.
Two quadratics have a root in common. How do I find it?
Subtract one equation from the other. The shared root satisfies both, so it satisfies their difference, and the x² terms cancel to leave a linear equation that hands the root over. Scale one equation first if the leading coefficients differ. Substitute the root back to find the unknown — the subtraction only gives a candidate.
How do I write the equation whose roots are transformed?
Never find the original roots. Work out the new sum and the new product from the old ones and write x² − (new sum)x + (new product) = 0. Squaring the roots squares the product; taking reciprocals turns the sum into (α+β)/αβ; shifting each root by h adds 2h to the sum.
Why are linear equations in two variables part of this chapter?
Because the standard textbooks place consistency of linear systems here, and the tests follow that split. Compare the three ratios: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ gives exactly one solution; all three equal gives infinitely many; the first two equal with the third different gives no solution at all.
