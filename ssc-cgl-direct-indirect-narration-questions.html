Every narration question asks one thing: which option reports exactly what the speaker said? In direct speech the speaker’s words sit inside inverted commas. In indirect (reported) speech the inverted commas go, the sentence is joined to the reporting verb with that, if/whether, a Wh- word or to, and the tense, pronouns and time words shift to suit the moment of reporting.

Marks are lost in three places. The first is the tense — forgetting to move “is” to “was” or “has” to “had” after a past reporting verb, or shifting a universal truth that should stay in the present. The second is the pronoun — “I” becomes the speaker and “you” becomes the listener, not the other way round. The third is the sentence type: a question takes “asked” and statement word order, a command takes “to”, and “that” never comes before a question.

How Many Direct and Indirect Speech Questions Are Asked in SSC CGL?

Narration is a regular part of the English section — usually one question in an SSC CGL Tier I shift, sometimes two, and a few more in CHSL and the Tier II English paper. Like voice, it is one of the most scoring questions in the section, because the rules never change. The kinds of sentence that come up are listed below, most common first.

What You Will Practice in the Direct & Indirect Narration Chapter

Statements and tense changes

  • Simple, continuous and perfect tenses after a past reporting verb (is → was, has → had, wrote → had written)
  • Modals: will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to; would, could, should, might and ought to stay the same
  • No tense change after says / will say, and for universal truths, scientific facts and habitual facts
  • Pronouns and time and place words: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the previous day, ago → before, here → there, this → that

Questions, commands and other sentence types

  • Yes/no questions → asked if / whether + statement order; Wh- questions → asked + Wh- word + statement order
  • Commands → ordered / told + to; negative commands → not to; please → requested + to
  • “Let’s” → suggested that they should; “Let me” → requested to let / allow
  • Exclamations → exclaimed with joy / sorrow that; wishes and prayers → wished / prayed that … might
  • Good morning → wished; Thank you → thanked; Sorry → apologised; Yes / No → replied in the affirmative / negative

Level 2 extras

  • Conditionals, “I wish I were”, had better, used to and need not
  • Vocatives (Sir, Friends, Boys) and mixed sentences joined with “and added that” or “and asked”

If the reporting verb is in the present or future (says, will say), or the words are a universal truth, the tense does not change at all.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Keeping the question form. In indirect speech a question becomes a statement: “asked where I was going”, not “where was I going” — and no question mark.
  • Using “that” or “told” with a question. Questions take “asked” with if, whether or the Wh- word — never “asked that”.
  • Mixing up the pronouns. The first person changes to the speaker, the second person to the listener, and the third person stays as it is.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Direct & Indirect Narration

Q1. Change into indirect speech: She said to me, “I am leaving for Pune tomorrow.”
(a) She told me that she is leaving for Pune tomorrow.
(b) She told me that she was leaving for Pune the next day.
(c) She said to me that I was leaving for Pune the next day.
(d) She told me that she was leaving for Pune tomorrow.
“Said to” becomes “told”, “am leaving” becomes “was leaving”, “I” becomes “she” and “tomorrow” becomes “the next day”. Answer: (b)
Q2. Change into indirect speech: He said to the boy, “Where do you live?”
(a) He asked the boy where did he live.
(b) He told the boy where he lived.
(c) He asked the boy that where he lived.
(d) He asked the boy where he lived.
A question takes “asked” and statement word order, so “do you live” becomes “he lived” with no “that” and no question mark. Answer: (d)
Q3. Change into indirect speech: The teacher said to the students, “Do not make a noise.”
(a) The teacher ordered the students not to make a noise.
(b) The teacher ordered the students to not make a noise.
(c) The teacher told the students that do not make a noise.
(d) The teacher ordered the students that they did not make a noise.
A negative command becomes ordered / told + object + not to + verb. Answer: (a)
Q4. Change into direct speech: He told me that he had seen the film the previous day.
(a) He said to me, “I had seen the film the previous day.”
(b) He said to me, “He saw the film yesterday.”
(c) He said to me, “I saw the film yesterday.”
(d) He told me, “I saw the film yesterday.”
Going back to direct speech, “told” becomes “said to”, “he” becomes “I”, “had seen” becomes “saw” and “the previous day” becomes “yesterday”. Answer: (c)

How to Prepare Direct & Indirect Narration for SSC CGL

  1. Look at the reporting verb first. Past (said) means the tense shifts back; present or future (says, will say) means it stays — that alone removes two options in most questions.
  2. Name the sentence type. Statement → that; yes/no question → if / whether; Wh- question → the Wh- word; command → to; exclamation → exclaimed that.
  3. Learn the tense-change table by heart. One step back for each tense; past perfect and would, could, should, might stay as they are.
  4. Change pronouns with the SON rule. First person → Speaker, second person → Object (the listener), third person → No change.
  5. Check every time and place word. Today, tomorrow, yesterday, now, here, this and ago almost always change — and the options often differ only there.
  6. Do Level 1 untimed first, then with the 15-minute clock. Move to Level 2 once Level 1 is steady, and then check yourself on a previous year paper.

SSC CGL Direct & Indirect Narration — Frequently Asked Questions

How many direct and indirect speech questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one in an SSC CGL Tier I shift, sometimes two, and a few more in CHSL and the Tier II English paper. Reported-speech errors also appear inside Spot the Error and Sentence Improvement questions.
What is the basic rule for changing direct speech into indirect speech?
Remove the inverted commas, join the reported words with 'that' (statements), 'if/whether' or a Wh- word (questions) or 'to' (commands), change the pronouns to suit the speaker and listener, and, if the reporting verb is in the past, move every tense one step back — is to was, has to had, will to would.
When does the tense not change in indirect speech?
When the reporting verb is in the present or future (says, will say), and when the reported words are a universal truth, a scientific fact or a habitual fact. Past perfect, would, could, should, might and ought to also stay as they are.
How are questions changed into indirect speech?
'Said to' becomes 'asked'. A yes/no question is joined with 'if' or 'whether'; a Wh- question keeps its Wh- word. The question becomes a statement — subject before verb, do/does/did removed — and the question mark goes. 'That' is never used before a reported question.
How are commands and requests reported?
A command becomes ordered, commanded or told + listener + to + verb; a negative command takes 'not to'. A request with 'please' becomes 'requested … to', and 'please' is dropped. 'Let's' becomes 'suggested that they should'.
How many questions are in each test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the tests in English or Hindi?
The direct and indirect narration tests are in English, as the English Language section is in SSC CGL and CHSL. Every solution names the reporting verb, states the rule, converts the sentence word by word and explains why each wrong option fails, in simple English.
What is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2?
Level 1 is set at real SSC CGL difficulty — every tense change, modals, questions, commands, requests, exclamations and time words, with the classic SSC wrong options. Level 2 is harder than the exam: conditionals, used to, had better and need not, vocatives, historical facts and mixed two-part sentences, where each wrong option differs from the answer by one small detail.
