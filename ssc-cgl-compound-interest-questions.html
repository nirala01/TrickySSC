Compound interest is simple interest with one change, and the whole chapter follows from it: the interest of each year is added to the sum, so the next year earns on that larger sum too. Write the year's growth as a multiplying factor — 1 + R/100 — and almost every question in the chapter becomes a matter of applying that factor the right number of times, or dividing it out the right number of times.

The marks go on two things. The first is the conversion: when interest is added twice a year the rate is halved and the periods doubled, and 18 months is three half-year periods, not one and a half years. The second is the shape of the interest itself — two years at R% do not add 2R% to the sum but a little more, and that little more is exactly what every CI–SI difference question is about. Each test puts twenty-five compound interest questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes the recurring shapes visible.

The two difference rows are the ones to hold on to. Over two years the whole gap between compound and simple interest is one year's interest on the first year's interest, which is why P(R/100)² is such a small number — and why SSC likes giving it and asking for the sum.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Not converting when the compounding is not yearly. Half-yearly means the rate is halved and the periods doubled — 20% per annum for 2 years is four periods at 10%, not two at 20% or two at 10%. Quarterly quarters the rate and takes four times the periods.
  • Treating two years as 2R% of the sum. Two years at 10% add 21%, not 20%; at 20% they add 44%, not 40%. The extra is the interest on the first year's interest, and every CI–SI question is built on it.
  • Working a single year's interest on the original sum. The interest of the third year is the rate applied to the amount standing when the third year begins, not to the sum. Each year's interest is larger than the one before.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Compound Interest

Q1. Find the compound interest on ₹12,000 for 2 years at 10% per annum, compounded yearly.
The yearly factor is 1.1, so two years multiply by 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.21. The amount is 12000 × 1.21 = 14,520, so the interest is 14520 − 12000 = 2,520. Year by year it reads the same way: 10% of 12,000 is 1,200, taking the sum to 13,200, and 10% of 13,200 is 1,320 — together 2,520. Simple interest would have given only ₹2,400 over the same two years, and the extra ₹120 is the second year's interest on the first year's 1,200. Answer: ₹2,520
Q2. Find the compound interest on ₹8,000 for 1 year at 20% per annum, compounded half-yearly.
Halve the rate and double the periods: 10% per half year, two periods. The factor is 1.1² = 1.21, so the amount is 8000 × 1.21 = 9,680. Period by period: 10% of 8,000 is 800, taking the sum to 8,800, then 10% of 8,800 is 880. Using 20% for each half year would give ₹3,520 — far too much. Answer: ₹1,680
Q3. What is the difference between the compound interest and the simple interest on ₹15,000 for 2 years at 10% per annum?
Use D = P(R/100)² = 15000 × (0.1)² = 15000 × 0.01. Checking it the long way: SI is 15000 × 10 × 2 / 100 = 3,000 and CI is 15000(1.21 − 1) = 3,150. The gap is the second year's interest on the first year's 1,500. Answer: ₹150
Q4. A sum amounts to ₹5,324 in 3 years at 10% per annum, compounded yearly. Find the sum.
Run the factor backwards. Three years at 10% multiply by 1.1³ = 1.331, so the sum is 5324 / 1.331. Do not subtract the interest as though it were simple — under compounding the yearly interest is not a fixed amount. Forwards it reads 4000 → 4400 → 4840 → 5324. Answer: ₹4,000

How to Prepare Compound Interest for SSC CGL

  1. Do percentage and simple interest first. Every rate here is a percentage, and the CI–SI questions — a good share of the chapter — cannot be attempted without the simple interest formula.
  2. Learn the factors, not the formula. Keep 1.1² = 1.21, 1.1³ = 1.331, 1.2² = 1.44, 1.2³ = 1.728, 1.25² = 1.5625 and 1.05² = 1.1025 in your head. Most Tier I questions are one multiplication once you know the factor.
  3. Write awkward rates as fractions. 12½% = 1/8 so the factor is 9/8; 16⅔% = 1/6 gives 7/6; 25% gives 5/4. Fractions keep the arithmetic exact and the cancelling obvious.
  4. Convert the compounding before anything else. Halve and double for half-yearly, quarter and quadruple for quarterly, and count the periods in the time given rather than the years.
  5. Memorise the two difference formulas. P(R/100)² for two years and P(R/100)²(3 + R/100) for three. They are asked in both directions and they save a full minute each time.
  6. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right factor and the right number of periods, not speed.
  7. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. A Tier I compound interest question that takes more than about fifty seconds usually means the factor was rebuilt from scratch instead of recalled.
  8. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 1 is consistent under the clock, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Compound Interest — Frequently Asked Questions

How many compound interest questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one to two questions in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and one to two in Tier II Paper I. Counted together with simple interest, which shares every letter of the formula, the pair is worth three to five marks in most shifts, and questions that compare the two are set almost every cycle.
What topics come under compound interest for SSC CGL?
The amount and the interest for whole years, working back from an amount or an interest figure to the sum, half-yearly and quarterly compounding, conversion periods of four and eight months, part-year terms, a different rate in each year, nominal and effective rates, the rate or the time from a growth multiple, the difference between compound and simple interest over two and three years, the interest of one particular year, equal instalments, early settlement of a debt, repeated deposits, and depreciation or population growth at a compound rate.
What is the formula for compound interest?
A = P(1 + R/100)^n and CI = A − P, where P is the sum, R the rate per cent per annum and n the number of years. The bracket is the multiplying factor: the sum is multiplied by it once for every year. For half-yearly compounding halve the rate and double the periods, and for quarterly compounding quarter the rate and take four times the periods.
How many questions are in each compound interest practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the compound interest tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Are these SSC CGL compound interest tests free?
Yes, every test is free. You do need a free TrickySSC account to start one — tapping a test opens a sign-in box on the page itself, with Google or a mobile OTP, and the test begins the moment you are signed in.
What is the difference between compound interest and simple interest for 2 years?
Over two years the gap is exactly one year's interest on the first year's interest, which works out to P × (R/100)². At 10% on ₹15,000 that is ₹150. Over three years the gap is P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100). Both formulas are worth memorising, because SSC sets them in both directions — sometimes the gap is given and the sum is wanted.
Which chapters should I study along with compound interest?
Simple interest first, because the two use the same principal, rate and time and are regularly compared in one question. Percentage before that, since every rate here is a percentage and the multiplying factor is a percentage idea. Profit and loss and discount share the commercial vocabulary, and depreciation questions in this chapter are the same arithmetic run downwards.
