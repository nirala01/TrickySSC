A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind and carries no units, which is why it travels so far in this syllabus. Write the quantities as 3k and 5k and a ratio stops being a comparison and becomes arithmetic: one unknown, one equation, and every share in the question expressed in the same part. A proportion is the statement that two ratios are equal, and it hands over exactly one usable fact — ad = bc, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means. Mean proportional, third proportional and fourth proportional are that one fact with a different term missing.

The marks go on two habits. The first is reading part to part correctly: 3 : 2 means 3 out of every 5, not 3 out of every 2, and the commonest lost mark in the chapter is a part-to-part ratio used as a fraction of the whole. The second is knowing what survives a change — multiplying every term by the same number leaves a ratio alone, while adding or subtracting the same amount from every term does not. Each test puts twenty-five ratio questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those recurring shapes visible.

The middle rows are the ones to hold on to. Mean, third and fourth proportional are not three formulas but one — ad = bc with a different term missing each time — and every componendo question is that same equation rearranged.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Reading a part-to-part ratio as a share of the whole. 3 : 2 means 3 out of every 5, not 3 out of every 2; 1 : 4 means one in five. Almost every cross-classified question — boys and girls inside two faculties, men and women inside two grades — is set to catch exactly this.
  • Adding ratio terms across two groups of different sizes. If one shop sells 250 items in the ratio 3 : 2 and another sells 270 in the ratio 5 : 4, the overall ratio is not 8 : 6. A part in the first shop is 50 items and a part in the second is 30, so both must become actual counts before anything is added.
  • Assuming a flat addition leaves a ratio alone. Multiplying every term by the same number keeps a ratio; adding or subtracting the same amount from every term does not. Rs 300 added to shares of 2 : 3 : 4 makes them 23 : 33 : 43, which is a flatter ratio — a common addition always pulls the terms closer together.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Ratio and Proportion

Q1. If A : B = 3 : 4 and B : C = 8 : 9, find A : B : C.
The two ratios meet at B, so B must carry the same number in both. It is 4 in the first and 8 in the second, so double the first: A : B = 6 : 8. The chain is then 6 : 8 : 9, and no number divides all three. Hanging the 9 straight onto 3 : 4 to get 3 : 4 : 9 is the slip — the scaling has to happen before anything is attached. Answer: 6 : 8 : 9
Q2. Two numbers are in the ratio 3 : 5. When 10 is added to each, the ratio becomes 5 : 7. Find the smaller number.
Write them as 3k and 5k. Adding a constant changes the numbers but never k, so (3k + 10)/(5k + 10) = 5/7. Cross-multiplying gives 21k + 70 = 25k + 50, so 4k = 20 and k = 5. The numbers are 15 and 25, and adding 10 to each gives 25 and 35, which is 5 : 7. Answer: 15
Q3. Find the mean proportional between 8 and 50, and the third proportional to 5 and 20.
The mean proportional is √(8 × 50) = √400 = 20, and it checks out as 8 : 20 = 20 : 50, both sides being 2 : 5. The third proportional is 20²/5 = 80, checking as 5 : 20 = 20 : 80, both 1 : 4. Both come from the same rule, b² = ac — in the first the middle term was missing, in the second an end term was. Answer: 20 and 80
Q4. The incomes of two men are in the ratio 4 : 5 and their expenditures in the ratio 3 : 4. If each saves ₹4,000, what is the second man's income?
The two ratios are unconnected, so they need separate multipliers: incomes 4x and 5x, expenditures 3y and 4y. Savings give 4x − 3y = 4000 and 5x − 4y = 4000. Multiplying the first by 4 and the second by 3 and subtracting leaves x = 4000, so the incomes are ₹16,000 and ₹20,000, the expenditures ₹12,000 and ₹16,000. Using one multiplier for both ratios is the mistake here. Answer: ₹20,000

How to Prepare Ratio and Proportion for SSC CGL

  1. Do percentage first. A percentage is a ratio out of a hundred, and the income–expenditure and fractional-change questions in this chapter read much more easily once percentage is fluent.
  2. Put every question on one multiplier. Writing the quantities as 3k and 5k turns a comparison into one unknown and one equation. Two unconnected ratios in the same question, though, need two multipliers — x and y, never k twice.
  3. Learn ad = bc instead of three formulas. Mean proportional, third proportional and fourth proportional are the same rule with a different term missing, and so is every componendo question.
  4. Say the part-to-whole out loud. Before using 3 : 2, say “three out of five”. That one habit removes the most expensive mistake in the chapter.
  5. Know what survives a change. A common multiplier keeps a ratio; a common addition or subtraction does not. If the question adds a flat amount, the actual numbers must be found first.
  6. Learn the ratio words. Duplicate is squaring, sub-duplicate is the square root, triplicate is cubing, sub-triplicate is the cube root, and compound is ac : bd. These questions are pure vocabulary — the arithmetic is trivial once the word is read correctly.
  7. For variation, fix the constant first. Write A = kB/C, find k from the complete reading, and only then substitute — whichever letter is the unknown. A factor on a squared quantity enters squared, and on a quantity underneath it inverts.
  8. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  9. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. A Tier I ratio question that takes more than about forty seconds usually means the parts were not set up on one multiplier at the start.
  10. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 1 is consistent under the clock, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Ratio and Proportion — Frequently Asked Questions

How many ratio and proportion questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one to two questions in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and two to three in Tier II Paper I. Counted with the chapters built on it — partnership, average, mixture and alligation, and the income–expenditure questions — ratio reasoning is worth far more than its own count, because turning the words into a ratio is the first step in solving them.
What topics come under ratio and proportion for SSC CGL?
Dividing a total in a given ratio, joining two or three ratios into one chain, finding a term when one member is known, a constant added to or taken from both terms, unequal changes to two groups, ratios written as fractions or decimals, mean proportional, third proportional and fourth proportional, continued proportion, componendo and dividendo, duplicate and sub-duplicate and triplicate ratios, compound ratio, direct and inverse and joint variation, ratios of areas and volumes, coins of different denominations, income–expenditure–savings, mixtures and replacement, transfers between two groups, and groups classified two ways at once.
What is the difference between ratio and proportion?
A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind, written a : b, and it has no units. A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal, written a : b = c : d, and it gives one usable equation: ad = bc, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means. Every mean, third and fourth proportional question is that one equation with a different term missing.
How do I find the mean proportional and the third proportional?
The mean proportional between a and b is √(ab), because a : m = m : b gives m² = ab. The third proportional to a and b is b²/a, from a : b = b : c. The fourth proportional to a, b and c is bc/a. All three come from the same rule, so it is worth learning ad = bc rather than three separate formulas. Note that order matters for a third proportional but not for a mean proportional.

What does duplicate ratio mean, and how is it different from compound ratio?
The duplicate ratio of a : b is a² : b² — each term squared. Sub-duplicate is the square root of each term, triplicate is each term cubed and sub-triplicate is the cube root. A compound ratio is different: it combines two separate ratios into one by multiplying the first terms together and the second terms together, so a : b compounded with c : d is ac : bd. These questions are mostly vocabulary — the arithmetic is easy once the word is read correctly.
Which chapters should I study along with ratio and proportion?
Percentage first, since a percentage is a ratio out of a hundred. Then partnership, average and mixture and alligation, which are ratio chapters wearing different words — profit shared in the ratio of capital and time, a total split back out of an average, and two strengths blended by the alligation rule. Time and work and time–speed–distance lean on ratio reasoning throughout as well.
