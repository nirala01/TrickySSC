Almost every question in this chapter rests on one line: distance = speed × time. What makes it hard is everything around that line — units that must match, speeds that combine as a relative speed, and lengths that a train has to cover before it is past anything.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is skipping the 5/18 conversion — a 150 m train at 54 km/h passes a pole in 10 seconds, not in 150 ÷ 54 of anything. The second is taking the plain middle of two speeds: going at 40 km/h and coming back at 60 km/h gives an average speed of 48 km/h, not 50. The third is using the wrong relative speed or the wrong distance — subtracting speeds for trains that approach each other, or forgetting one of the two lengths. The first three rows carry the chapter. Most of the others are those three applied with the right relative speed and the right distance.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Mixing units. A length in metres and a time in seconds need a speed in m/s. Multiply km/h by 5/18 first; leaving it out puts the answer off by a factor of 3.6, and that wrong value is almost always one of the options.
  • Averaging speeds directly. Average speed is total distance over total time. For equal distances at 40 and 60 km/h it is 2 × 40 × 60 ÷ 100 = 48 km/h; the plain middle, 50, is the trap.
  • Picking the wrong distance or relative speed. Add speeds for bodies moving towards each other and subtract them for bodies moving the same way. A train must cover its own length past a pole, and both lengths past another train.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Time, Speed and Distance

Q1. A man drives to a town at 40 km/h and returns along the same road at 60 km/h. What is his average speed for the round trip?
The two legs are equal distances, so average speed = 2 × 40 × 60 ÷ (40 + 60) = 4800 ÷ 100 = 48 km/h. Check with a 120 km road: 3 hours out and 2 hours back, 240 km in 5 hours. The plain middle, 50 km/h, is the usual wrong option. Answer: 48 km/h
Q2. A 150 m long train running at 72 km/h crosses a platform 250 m long. How long does it take?
The train must cover its own length and the platform: 150 + 250 = 400 m. Its speed is 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s, so the time is 400 ÷ 20 = 20 seconds. Using only the platform length gives 12.5 seconds — a common wrong option. Answer: 20 seconds
Q3. Two trains, 120 m and 180 m long, run towards each other on parallel tracks at 50 km/h and 58 km/h. How long do they take to cross each other completely?
Moving towards each other, the relative speed is the sum: 50 + 58 = 108 km/h = 30 m/s. They must slide past both lengths, 120 + 180 = 300 m, so the time is 300 ÷ 30 = 10 seconds. Using the difference of speeds would give 135 seconds. Answer: 10 seconds
Q4. Walking at 4 km/h, a student reaches school 10 minutes late; walking at 5 km/h, she reaches 5 minutes early. How far is the school?
The school bell lies between the two arrivals, so the two walking times differ by 10 + 5 = 15 minutes. For the same distance D, D(1/4 − 1/5) = 15/60, so D ÷ 20 = 1/4 and D = 5 km. Check: 75 minutes against 60 minutes. Taking the gap as 10 − 5 is the trap. Answer: 5 km

How to Prepare Time, Speed and Distance for SSC CGL

  1. Fix the units first. Decide whether the question lives in km and hours or in metres and seconds, and convert every speed before you use it.
  2. Draw the situation. A line with two dots and arrows shows at once whether the speeds add or subtract, and what distance has to be closed.
  3. Name the relative speed. Same direction, subtract; towards or away from each other, add. Write it down before dividing.
  4. For trains, list the lengths. A pole or a person adds nothing; a platform, bridge or tunnel adds its own length; another train adds its length too.
  5. Never average speeds directly. Use total distance over total time, or the 2ab ÷ (a + b) short cut for equal distances.
  6. For late and early questions, add the minutes. One arrival is before the fixed time and one after, so the two walking times differ by the sum.
  7. Keep halts in the time, not the distance. A stop costs minutes but covers no ground.
  8. Underline what is asked. A speed, a length, a clock time, the time after a meeting or the distance still to go are all different answers.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Time, Speed and Distance — Frequently Asked Questions

How many time, speed and distance questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one or two in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and two to three in Tier II Paper I, with trains and boats counted alongside. Most of them are one-line relative-speed or average-speed questions that a prepared candidate can finish in under a minute.
What topics come under time, speed and distance for SSC CGL?
Converting between km/h and m/s; speed ratios and inverse time ratios; average speed over equal distances and equal times; late and early arrivals; chasing, meeting and delayed starts; turn-back meetings; the time after two travellers meet; buses met on the road; trains crossing poles, people, platforms, bridges and other trains; stoppages; wheel turns and clock hands; sound, echo and rail clicks; and walking inside a moving train.
How do I convert km/h to m/s quickly?
Multiply by 5/18 to go from km/h to m/s, and by 18/5 to go back. So 72 km/h is 20 m/s and 15 m/s is 54 km/h. Always convert before you add or subtract speeds, and before you use a length in metres with a time in seconds.
Why is average speed not the plain middle of the two speeds?
Because average speed is total distance divided by total time, and the slower stretch takes longer. For equal distances at a and b it is 2ab ÷ (a + b): going at 40 km/h and returning at 60 km/h gives 48 km/h, not 50. Only when equal times are spent at each speed is the answer the plain middle, (a + b) ÷ 2.
What is relative speed?
It is how fast one moving body gains on, or closes in on, another. For two bodies moving the same way it is the difference of their speeds; for two bodies moving towards each other, or away from each other, it is the sum. Catch time is the gap divided by the difference; meeting time is the gap divided by the sum.
How do train crossing questions work?
A train passing a pole or a standing man covers only its own length. Passing a platform, bridge or tunnel it covers its length plus that structure. Passing another train it covers both lengths, at the relative speed: the difference of speeds for the same direction and the sum for opposite directions.
How many questions are in each time, speed and distance practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the time, speed and distance tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with time, speed and distance?
Ratio and proportion first, because speed, time and distance questions are ratio questions in disguise. Averages helps with average speed, time and work uses the same inverse-proportion idea, and quadratic equations help with the questions where a speed change saves a stated time.
