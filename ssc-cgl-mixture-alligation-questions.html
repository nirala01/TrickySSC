Mixture and alligation looks like several chapters but runs on two ideas. The first is that the liquid you are not touching stays fixed: pour in water and the milk does not change; draw off part of a mixture and every liquid falls by the same fraction. The second is alligation, the balancing rule for two ingredients at different prices or strengths: cheaper : dearer = (dearer − mean) : (mean − cheaper). Nearly every question in the paper is one of these two ideas, or both in a row.

The marks are lost in two places. The first is adding ratio numbers instead of quantities — two mixtures in the ratios 3 : 2 and 1 : 4 do not combine to 4 : 6 unless the two vessels hold exactly the same number of parts, and the wrong option for that slip is on the paper almost every time. The second is subtracting the same amount at every replacement: once water has gone in, each later draw takes out some water too, so the original liquid falls by a fraction, not by a fixed number of litres. Each test puts twenty-five mixture questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes both habits automatic.

Two rows do most of the work: the alligation rule, and “a draw multiplies every liquid by the same fraction”. Most of the others are those two applied in a particular order.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Adding ratio numbers instead of quantities. Before combining two mixtures, turn each ratio into litres of each liquid, then add like with like. Adding the ratio terms is right only when both vessels hold the same number of parts, and the option built on that slip is offered almost every time.
  • Taking the same amount out at every replacement. From a 50-litre can of milk, three draws of 10 litres replaced with water leave 50 × (4/5)³ = 25.6 litres of milk, not 20. After the first draw, each draw removes some water as well.
  • Setting up alligation the wrong way round — or with the wrong mean. The cheaper ingredient is matched with (dearer − mean), so the ingredient nearer the mean gets the larger share. In a gain question the mean is the cost of the blend, found by dividing the selling rate by (1 + gain), never the selling rate itself.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Mixture and Alligation

Q1. In what ratio must rice costing Rs 42 per kg be mixed with rice costing Rs 60 per kg so that the mixture costs Rs 48 per kg?
Use alligation, matching the cheaper rice with the gap on the other side: cheaper : dearer = (60 − 48) : (48 − 42) = 12 : 6 = 2 : 1. Check: 2 kg at Rs 42 and 1 kg at Rs 60 cost 84 + 60 = 144 rupees for 3 kg, which is Rs 48 per kg. Writing the differences the other way gives 1 : 2 — but Rs 48 is nearer Rs 42, so the cheaper rice must be the larger share. Answer: 2 : 1
Q2. A 45-litre mixture has milk and water in the ratio 7 : 2. How much water must be added to make the ratio 5 : 2?
Adding water leaves the milk fixed at (7/9) × 45 = 35 litres, and the water is 10 litres. For milk : water = 5 : 2, the water must be (2/5) × 35 = 14 litres, so 14 − 10 = 4 litres are added. Check: 35 : 14 = 5 : 2. It is tempting to think no water is needed because the water’s ratio number is 2 in both ratios, but the milk’s number changed from 7 to 5; rewrite 5 : 2 as 7 : 2.8 and the water visibly rises. Answer: 4 litres
Q3. An 80-litre drum is full of pure juice. Twenty litres are drawn off and replaced with water, and this is done once more. How much juice is left?
Each draw removes a quarter of whatever is in the drum, so each round keeps 1 − 20/80 = 3/4 of the juice. After two rounds: 80 × (3/4)² = 45 litres. Step by step: 80 becomes 60, then the second draw takes a quarter of 60, leaving 45. Subtracting 20 litres twice gives 40 — wrong, because the second draw is only three-quarters juice. Answer: 45 litres
Q4. A jar holds a 70% alcohol solution. Part of it is replaced with a 30% alcohol solution, and the jar is then 52% alcohol. What fraction of the jar was replaced?
If a fraction f is replaced, the new strength is (1 − f) × 70 + f × 30 = 52, which gives f = (70 − 52) / (70 − 30) = 18/40 = 9/20. Check: (11/20) × 70 + (9/20) × 30 = 38.5 + 13.5 = 52. The fraction kept is 11/20, and that is the usual wrong option. Answer: 9/20

How to Prepare Mixture and Alligation for SSC CGL

  1. Turn every ratio into litres first. Write the quantity of each liquid before doing anything else. Ratios cannot be added across vessels; litres can.
  2. Ask which liquid is not being touched. When only water goes in, the milk is fixed; when only milk goes in, the water is fixed. Build the target ratio around the fixed liquid and the answer is one line.
  3. Treat a draw as a fraction, not a number. Drawing x litres from V multiplies every liquid by 1 − x/V. The liquid poured back is then added to its own amount only.
  4. Learn the alligation cross cold. cheaper : dearer = (dearer − mean) : (mean − cheaper). The same cross works for prices, percentage strengths, milk shares written as fractions, carats and relative heaviness.
  5. Check the answer’s direction. The ingredient whose value is nearer the mean always gets the larger share; if your ratio says otherwise, the differences are the wrong way round.
  6. For replacement by another mixture, use f = (a − c)/(a − b). It gives the fraction replaced in one step, whether the strengths are percentages or shares.
  7. For repeated replacement, use powers. V(1 − x/V)ⁿ for the original liquid left; when the draws differ, multiply their separate fractions instead.
  8. In gain questions, find the cost of the blend first. Divide the selling rate by (1 + gain), then alligate against that cost.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Mixture and Alligation — Frequently Asked Questions

How many mixture and alligation questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually zero to one question in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and one to two in Tier II Paper I. Alligation also settles weighted-average questions and the mix-and-sell-at-a-gain problems set under profit and loss, so the chapter is worth more than its direct count.
What topics come under mixture and alligation for SSC CGL?
Water or a pure liquid added to change a ratio or a percentage; part of a mixture removed and replaced with a pure liquid or another mixture; two or three mixtures combined in given quantities or equal volumes; the ratio in which two mixtures, prices or gold grades must be mixed; the cost of a blend and the price of a third variety; free water mixed into milk; blends sold at a gain; equal money spent on two varieties; repeated replacement and its reverse; and transfers between two containers.
What is the rule of alligation?
When two ingredients with values a (cheaper) and b (dearer) are mixed to give a mean value m, they must be taken in the ratio cheaper : dearer = (b − m) : (m − a). The values can be prices per kg, percentage strengths, milk shares or gold carats. The ingredient whose value is nearer the mean always gets the larger share.
What is the formula for repeated replacement?
If x litres are drawn from a full V-litre vessel and replaced with water, and this is done n times, the original liquid left is V × (1 − x/V)^n. The liquid falls by a fraction each time, not by a fixed x litres, because every draw after the first also removes some water.
How do I find the fraction replaced when a mixture is replaced by another mixture?
If a solution of strength a is partly replaced by one of strength b and the result has strength c, the fraction replaced is (a − c) ÷ (a − b). For example, a 70% solution partly replaced by a 30% one to give 52% has 18/40 = 9/20 of it replaced.
Why can't I just add the ratio numbers when two mixtures are combined?
Because the two vessels usually hold different numbers of parts. Mixtures in the ratios 3 : 2 and 1 : 3 combined in equal volumes give 17 : 23, not 4 : 5. Convert each ratio into litres of each liquid, add like with like, and only then form the new ratio.
How many questions are in each mixture and alligation practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the mixture and alligation tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with mixture and alligation?
Average first, because alligation is the weighted-average idea written as a ratio. Ratio and proportion supplies the part-to-litre conversions, percentage supplies the strengths, and profit and loss uses alligation for the blend-and-sell questions. Doing average, ratio, mixture and profit and loss as one block saves a good deal of time.
