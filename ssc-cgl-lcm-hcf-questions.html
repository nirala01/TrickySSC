The single fastest filter in this chapter: the word greatest almost always means HCF and the word least almost always means LCM — except in remainder-on-division questions, where the least number still comes from the LCM.

The product rule is for two numbers only. For 2, 2 and 2 the HCF is 2 and the LCM is 2, so HCF × LCM is 4 while the product is 8.

Remainder patterns — the three forms

ConditionForm of the numberExample
Divisible by a, b, cL × kLeast is L; greatest three-digit is 999 minus its remainder on L
Same remainder r on a, b, cL × k + rRemainder 5 on 6, 9, 12 → 36k + 5 → 41, 77, 113 …
Divisor minus remainder is the same d each timeL × k − dRemainders 1, 3, 5 on 4, 6, 8 → 24k − 3 → 21, 45, 69 …

Least number to subtract from N to reach a common multiple = the remainder of N on L; least to add = L minus that remainder. The two always sum to L.

Fractions and decimals

  • LCM of fractions = LCM of the numerators ÷ HCF of the denominators. HCF of fractions = HCF of the numerators ÷ LCM of the denominators.
  • Reduce every fraction first. 4/6, 6/9 and 10/15 are all 2/3, so their HCF is 2/3 — the rule applied without reducing gives 1/45.
  • Mixed numbers become improper fractions before either rule is used.
  • Decimals: pad to the same number of decimal places, drop the point, take the LCM or HCF of the whole numbers, then restore the same number of places. LCM(0.4, 0.06, 0.9) → 40, 6, 90 → 360 → 3.6.

Also worth memorising

  • HCF(ka, kb) = k × HCF(a, b) and LCM(ka, kb) = k × LCM(a, b) — a common factor scales both
  • HCF(a + b, b) = HCF(a, b) — the step that makes Euclid’s method work
  • Least perfect square divisible by a set = LCM × the primes carrying odd powers; for a cube, raise each power to the next multiple of 3
  • Numbers in the ratio a : b : c have HCF h and LCM h × LCM(a, b, c), so LCM ÷ HCF depends only on the ratio
  • 1/x + 1/y = (x + y) ÷ (xy), and xy = HCF × LCM
  • HCF(n, n + 1) = 1; HCF(n, n + 2) = 2 when n is even; LCM(n, n + 1) = n(n + 1)

Solved Examples — SSC CGL LCM & HCF

Q1. The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 180. If one number is 36, find the other.
For two numbers, product = HCF × LCM. So the other number is 12 × 180 ÷ 36 = 60. Check: HCF(36, 60) = 12 and LCM(36, 60) = 180. Answer: 60
Q2. Four bells toll at intervals of 4, 6, 9 and 12 seconds. They begin together at 12:00. How many times do they toll together up to 12:15, both ends included?
LCM(4, 6, 9, 12) = 36 seconds. The period is 15 × 60 = 900 seconds, and 900 ÷ 36 = 25 tolls after the start. Counting the toll at 12:00 gives 25 + 1. Answer: 26
Q3. Find the greatest number that divides 424, 984 and 1684 leaving the same remainder in each case.
The remainder is not stated, so take the differences: 984 − 424 = 560, 1684 − 984 = 700, 1684 − 424 = 1260. HCF(560, 700, 1260) = 140, and each number leaves remainder 4. Answer: 140
Q4. Which is the smallest multiple of 11 that leaves remainder 4 on division by 6, 8 and 9?
LCM(6, 8, 9) = 72, so the family is 72k + 4: 76, 148, 220, … The first multiple of 11 in it is 220 = 11 × 20. Answer: 220
Q5. Two numbers are in the ratio 8 : 11 and their HCF is 17. Find their LCM.
With 8 and 11 co-prime, the numbers are 17 × 8 = 136 and 17 × 11 = 187, and the LCM is 17 × 8 × 11 = 1496. Answer: 1496

How to Prepare LCM & HCF for SSC CGL

  1. Decide LCM or HCF before you write anything. Greatest that divides or measures is HCF; least that is divisible, or repeating events meeting again, is LCM. Getting this wrong costs the whole question.
  2. Learn Euclid’s method properly. For four-digit numbers it beats factorisation every time, and the same idea powers the difference trick in same-remainder questions.
  3. Memorise the product rule and its limit. a × b = HCF × LCM for two numbers, never for three.
  4. Convert units before you start. 3 m 60 cm is 360 cm, and 1 minute 20 seconds is 80 seconds. Mixed units are the commonest silent error in this chapter.
  5. Practise the co-prime split. Writing the numbers as ha and hb with a, b co-prime solves every “how many pairs”, “which ratio” and “largest difference” question in one move.
  6. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the rules. The aim is correct recall, not speed.
  7. Move to Level 2 with the 30-minute clock on. Level 2 chains two ideas per question, which is what a real Tier I shift does.
  8. Study it with number system and divisibility. Do number system and divisibility first — factorisation is the shared skill.
  9. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 2 is consistent, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL LCM & HCF — Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find the HCF of two large numbers quickly?
Use Euclid’s division method. Divide the larger by the smaller, then the divisor by the remainder, and repeat until the remainder is 0. The last non-zero remainder is the HCF — much faster than factorising four-digit numbers.
What is the relation between HCF, LCM and the two numbers?
For exactly two numbers, HCF × LCM = the product of the numbers, so any two of the four quantities give the third. It fails for three or more: 2, 4 and 8 have HCF 2 and LCM 8, but their product is 64.
How do I solve same remainder questions?
If the remainder is not stated, take the differences of the numbers and find the HCF of those differences. If it is stated, subtract it from each number first, then take the HCF. In both cases the divisor must exceed the remainder.
What is the LCM and HCF of fractions?
LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators ÷ HCF of denominators. HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators ÷ LCM of denominators. Reduce every fraction to lowest terms first, or the answer is wrong — 4/6, 6/9 and 10/15 are all 2/3.
When do I use LCM and when do I use HCF in word problems?
LCM for the least number divisible by everything, and for repeating events meeting again — bells, lights, runners. HCF for the greatest length that measures several lengths exactly, the longest equal pieces with nothing wasted, and the greatest number of identical packs.
How many LCM and HCF questions are asked in SSC CGL?
One to two in a typical Tier I shift and one to two in Tier II Paper I — usually a bells or remainder word problem, a greatest measuring length, or a pair given through their ratio. The chapter is short, so the return per hour is high.
