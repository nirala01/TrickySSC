Pipes and cisterns look like a chapter of their own, but they run on the time and work method with one extra rule. Take the tank’s capacity as the LCM of the given times, count each pipe’s efficiency as the units it moves in an hour, and add those efficiencies — with an outlet or a leak entered as a minus. Almost every question in the chapter is that one line, applied once or applied in stages.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is adding an outlet instead of subtracting it — an inlet of 8 hours with an outlet of 12 hours fills the tank in 24 hours, not in 4 hours 48 minutes. The second is the overshoot in pipes that take turns: the tank fills partway through a filling hour, before the emptying pipe gets another go, so dividing by the gain per pair always gives too much. The third is answering the wrong stretch of time — giving the time after a pipe was shut when the question counted from the start, or the reverse. Each test puts twenty-five pipe and cistern questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those checks automatic.

The first three rows carry the chapter. Most of the others are those three applied in a particular order.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Adding an outlet’s efficiency. An inlet of 8 hours and an outlet of 12 hours give 3 − 2 = 1 unit an hour on a 24-unit tank, so 24 hours. Adding them gives 4 hours 48 minutes, and that wrong value is almost always one of the options.
  • Overshooting in alternate-pipe questions. A tank that gains 1 unit per pair of hours does not take 2 × capacity hours: it fills during an inlet hour, before the outlet runs again. Stop the pairs once one more inlet hour can finish the job.
  • Answering the wrong stretch of time. Many questions have two phases. Check whether the question counts from the start, from the moment a pipe was shut, or asks for a clock time — and whether it wants the time a pipe ran or how early it stopped.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Pipe and Cistern

Q1. Pipe A can fill a tank in 12 hours and pipe B in 15 hours. How long will they take together?
Take the capacity as the LCM of 12 and 15, which is 60 units. A fills 60 ÷ 12 = 5 units an hour and B fills 60 ÷ 15 = 4. Together they fill 9 units an hour, so the tank takes 60 ÷ 9 = 6⅔ hours, that is 6 hours 40 minutes. Check: two pipes together must beat the faster pipe’s 12 hours, and they do. Answer: 6 hours 40 minutes
Q2. An inlet can fill a tank in 8 hours and an outlet can empty it in 12 hours. If both are opened on the empty tank, when will it be full?
Capacity = LCM(8, 12) = 24 units. The inlet adds 3 units an hour and the outlet removes 2, so the tank gains 3 − 2 = 1 unit an hour. It is full after 24 ÷ 1 = 24 hours. Adding the two efficiencies would give 4 hours 48 minutes — the usual wrong option. Answer: 24 hours
Q3. A pipe fills a tank in 6 hours. Because of a leak at the bottom it now takes 8 hours. How long would the leak alone take to empty the full tank?
Capacity = LCM(6, 8) = 24 units. Alone the pipe fills 4 units an hour; with the leak the tank gains only 3. So the leak removes 4 − 3 = 1 unit an hour and empties the tank in 24 hours. The short cut gives the same: 6 × 8 ÷ (8 − 6) = 24. Answer: 24 hours
Q4. An inlet fills a tank in 6 hours and an outlet empties it in 9 hours. Starting with the empty tank, they are run alternately for one hour each, the inlet first. When will the tank be full?
Capacity = LCM(6, 9) = 18 units; the inlet adds 3 and the outlet removes 2, so each two-hour pair gains 1 unit. The tank fills during an inlet hour, and that hour can add 3 units, so the pairs only need to reach 18 − 3 = 15 units: 15 pairs, or 30 hours. The 31st hour is the inlet’s, and it takes the tank from 15 to exactly 18. Dividing 18 by the pair gain and doubling gives 36 hours — the overshoot trap. Answer: 31 hours

How to Prepare Pipe and Cistern for SSC CGL

  1. Set the capacity first. Take the LCM of every time in the question, including any joint time, and work in units from then on.
  2. Give every pipe a sign. Write inlets with a plus and outlets and leaks with a minus before you add anything.
  3. Bring all times to one unit. Hours and minutes, or a mixed number such as 4½ hours, must be converted before the LCM.
  4. Split schedules into phases. Units in a phase are the net efficiency times its hours; the last phase is the units left divided by its net efficiency.
  5. Use the add-back trick for a pipe shut early. Pretend it ran to the end, add the units it missed to the capacity, and divide by the full efficiency.
  6. Walk through alternate-pipe questions. Whole pairs first, then hour by hour; the tank almost always fills partway through a filling hour.
  7. Keep litres and units apart. When a pipe is given in litres per minute, work the whole question in litres, and remember 1 m³ is 1000 litres.
  8. Underline what is asked. Total time, time after a change, a clock time, the time a pipe ran or how early it stopped are all different answers.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Pipe and Cistern — Frequently Asked Questions

How many pipe and cistern questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually none or one in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and one to two in Tier II Paper I, often paired with a time and work question. It is a short chapter with one method, so a prepared candidate can clear most of its questions in about a minute.
What topics come under pipes and cisterns for SSC CGL?
Pipes filling together; an inlet working against an outlet; many identical inlets and outlets; recovering one pipe from joint times; a pipe opened late or closed early, including clock-time answers; multi-stage schedules; pipes run alternately; leaks, including leaks that appear midway; capacity from a pipe given in litres; pair times such as A with B and B with C; the number of taps against the time; partial blockages; flow through pipes of different diameters; and tank volume and water level.
What is the LCM method for pipe and cistern questions?
Take the tank's capacity as the LCM of the given times and count it in units. Each pipe's efficiency is the number of units it moves in one hour, which is the capacity divided by its time. Efficiencies add, an outlet's efficiency is subtracted, and the filling time is the capacity divided by the net efficiency. It avoids fractions altogether.
How do I handle an outlet or a leak?
Treat it exactly like an inlet with a minus sign. If an inlet fills a tank in 8 hours and an outlet empties it in 12, take 24 units: the inlet adds 3 an hour, the outlet removes 2, so the tank gains 1 unit an hour and fills in 24 hours. A leak that makes a pipe take T2 hours instead of T1 empties the full tank on its own in T1 × T2 ÷ (T2 − T1) hours.
How is pipes and cisterns different from time and work?
The method is the same — an inlet is a worker, the tank is the job — with one addition: an outlet or a leak is a worker with negative efficiency. That single change is where most wrong options come from, because it is easy to add an outlet's efficiency instead of subtracting it.
Why do alternate-pipe questions go wrong so often?
Because the tank usually fills partway through a turn. If an inlet and an outlet take turns, the tank reaches full during an inlet hour, before the outlet gets another turn, so dividing the capacity by the gain per pair and doubling overshoots the answer. Run whole pairs only while the tank is safely short, then finish hour by hour.
How many questions are in each pipe and cistern practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the pipe and cistern tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with pipes and cisterns?
Time and work first, because it uses the same efficiency method. LCM and HCF makes the capacity step quick, and quadratic equations help with the questions that give a joint time and the gap between two pipes.
