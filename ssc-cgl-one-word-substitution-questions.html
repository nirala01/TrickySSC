Every one word substitution question asks one thing: which single word means exactly what this group of words says? Sometimes the group of words is given on its own (“A person who cannot read or write”); sometimes it sits underlined inside a sentence, and the word you choose has to replace it and keep the sentence correct.

Marks are lost in three places. The first is the look-alike — Hangar and Hanger, Potable and Portable, Emigrant and Immigrant. The second is the same family — four -cides or four -phobias where only the root tells them apart. The third is the near meaning — Atheist when the definition says “nothing can be known” (Agnostic), or Gourmet when it says “eats far too much” (Gourmand). Read every word of the definition before you choose.

How Many One Word Substitution Questions Are Asked in SSC CGL?

One word substitution is a fixed part of the English section — usually one or two questions in every SSC CGL Tier I shift, two or three in CHSL, and more in the Tier II English paper. SSC repeats its words more here than in any other English chapter, so a well-practised list turns directly into marks. The kinds of word that come up are listed below, most common first.

Kind of wordHow often
A person by belief or behaviour (Atheist, Optimist, Philanthropist, Iconoclast)The most common form
A profession or specialist (Cartographer, Orthodontist, Numismatist)Common
A place or building (Hangar, Apiary, Mint, Mortuary, Granary)Common
An adjective with in-, im-, il- or -ible / -able (Illegible, Inevitable, Impregnable)Common
Study of, government and killing (-logy, -cracy, -cide)Regular
Fears, manias, groups, sounds and time wordsOccasional

The same words also come back as synonyms and in cloze tests — Insolvent, Posthumous and Ostracise are favourites.

Knowing the root does not replace knowing the word — in Level 2 every option shares the suffix, and only the first part tells them apart.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Picking the look-alike. A hangar shelters aircraft; a hanger holds clothes. Potable water is safe to drink; a portable radio can be carried.
  • Picking the opposite. The option list almost always holds the reverse of the answer — Emigrant for Immigrant, Novice for Veteran, Optimist for Pessimist.
  • Stopping at half the definition. “A person who does not believe in God” is an Atheist; “a person who believes nothing can be known about God” is an Agnostic. The last few words decide it.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL One Word Substitution

Q1. Select the option that can be used as a one-word substitute for the given group of words.
A person who draws or makes maps
(a) Calligrapher
(b) Choreographer
(c) Cartographer
(d) Geographer
Carto (map) + grapher (one who draws). A geographer studies the earth but does not have to make maps; a calligrapher writes beautifully and a choreographer designs dances. Answer: (c)
Q2. Select the option that can be used as a one-word substitute for the underlined group of words.
Rohan grew up in Chennai and Delhi, so he is able to speak two languages fluently.
(a) Illiterate
(b) Bilingual
(c) Monolingual
(d) Multilingual
Bi (two) + lingual (language). Monolingual is one language and multilingual is many, so only Bilingual matches “two”. Answer: (b)
Q3. Select the option that can be used as a one-word substitute for the given group of words.
Eating both plants and animals as food
(a) Herbivorous
(b) Carnivorous
(c) Insectivorous
(d) Omnivorous
Omni (all) + vorous (eating). Herbivorous eats only plants, carnivorous only flesh and insectivorous only insects. Answer: (d)
Q4. Select the option that can be used as a one-word substitute for the given group of words.
The act of copying another person's work and passing it off as one's own
(a) Plagiarism
(b) Forgery
(c) Piracy
(d) Perjury
Plagiarism is presenting someone else's writing or ideas as your own. Forgery is faking a document or signature, piracy is copying and selling without permission, and perjury is lying in court under oath. Answer: (a)

How to Prepare One Word Substitution for SSC CGL

  1. Start with the previous year words. SSC repeats them more than anything else in English — Level 1 here is built on them.
  2. Learn in families, not alphabetically. All the -cides together, all the -cracies together, all the -phobias together; the root then tells them apart.
  3. Learn the opposite with the word. Optimist with Pessimist, Emigrant with Immigrant, Veteran with Novice — the opposite is almost always one of the options.
  4. Read the whole definition. The last few words usually decide between two close options — “without causing loss of consciousness”, “in a skilful and amusing way”.
  5. For sentence questions, check the grammar too. An adjective replaces an adjective phrase and a noun replaces a noun phrase — read the sentence again with your word in it.
  6. Revise for ten minutes a day. Do Level 1 untimed first, then with the 15-minute clock; move to Level 2 once Level 1 is steady, and then check yourself on a previous year paper.

SSC CGL One Word Substitution — Frequently Asked Questions

How many one word substitution questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one or two in every SSC CGL Tier I shift, two or three in CHSL, and a few more in the Tier II English paper. Because SSC repeats its words heavily, it is one of the quickest places in the English section to pick up marks.
Are one word substitution words repeated in SSC exams?
Yes, very often. Words such as Veteran, Hangar, Septuagenarian, Nostalgia, Iconoclast, Philanthropist and Insolvent have come back across many shifts and years. That is why 40% of the words in these tests are taken from SSC CGL and CHSL previous year papers.
What is the fastest way to learn one word substitution?
Learn words in families rather than one by one: -cide for killing, -cracy for government, -logy for study, -phobia for fear, -mania for craze, -ist for a person, and the in-, im- and ir- adjectives. Then practise against close options so that you learn the exact difference between look-alike words.
What question types are in these tests?
Two, exactly as in SSC papers: one word for a given group of words, and one word to replace the underlined group of words in a sentence. Level 1 has more of the first type; Level 2 has more sentence questions.
How many questions are in each test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the tests in English or Hindi?
The one word substitution tests are in English, as the English Language section is in SSC CGL and CHSL. Every solution explains the group of words, the root or suffix that builds the answer, and why each wrong option fails, in simple English.
What is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2?
Level 1 is set at real SSC CGL difficulty — common exam words with clear traps such as an opposite word or a look-alike spelling. Level 2 is harder than the exam: rarer words like Sororicide, Kakistocracy and Raconteur, where every wrong option comes from the same family and differs from the answer in one key point.
