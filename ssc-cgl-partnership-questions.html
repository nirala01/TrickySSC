Partnership is one rule wearing a great many costumes. Two or more people put money into a business, and the profit is divided in the ratio of capital multiplied by the time that capital stayed in. Write each partner's weight as C × t, reduce the weights to a ratio, and every question in the chapter becomes dividing a total in a given ratio — which is why it is worth doing straight after ratio and proportion.

Almost all the marks turn on two habits. The first is counting each partner's own months: a partner who joins four months after the start is in for eight, one who leaves at the end of the ninth month is in for nine, and a single year can hold three different counts. The second is noticing what leaves the profit before the division — a charity fraction, a working partner's salary, a management share — because the capital ratio applies to the remainder, never to the whole profit. Each test puts twenty-five partnership questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those two habits automatic.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Giving a late joiner twelve months. “B joined after 4 months” means B's money was in for eight; “A left at the end of the ninth month” means nine. The paper words this differently each time — after four months, four months later, in the fifth month — and the count has to be made from the partner's own entry to his own exit, not from the closing date.
  • Dividing the whole profit by the capitals when something has already left it. A charity fraction, a management share and a salary all come off the top; the capital ratio then applies to the remainder. In a question with a working partner, the number to split is (1 − f)P, and half the wrong options offered are exactly what you get from splitting P instead.
  • Not stacking successive differences. If the first invests Rs 6,000 more than the second and the second Rs 3,000 more than the third, the first is Rs 9,000 above the third, not Rs 6,000. The same trap appears with differences between shares, and the distractor for the unstacked reading is always on the paper.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Partnership

Q1. P invests Rs 12,000 for 8 months, Q invests Rs 15,000 for 6 months and R invests Rs 18,000 for 5 months. If the profit is Rs 27,600, find P's share.
Weight each partner by capital times months: P gives 12,000 × 8 = 96,000, Q gives 15,000 × 6 = 90,000 and R gives 18,000 × 5 = 90,000. The ratio 96 : 90 : 90 reduces to 16 : 15 : 15, so there are 46 parts and one part is 27,600 / 46 = Rs 600. P takes 16 parts. Notice that Q and R have different capitals but identical weights, which is why their shares tie. Answer: Rs 9,600
Q2. A starts a business with Rs 36,000 and B joins later with Rs 54,000. At the end of the year their profits are in the ratio 4 : 3. After how many months did B join?
A is in for the full year, so his weight is 36,000 × 12 = 4,32,000. Setting the weight ratio equal to the profit ratio, 4,32,000 / (54,000 × t) = 4/3, which gives 54,000t = 3,24,000 and t = 6 months. B's money was therefore in for 6 months of a 12-month year, so he joined 6 months after the start. Always finish by subtracting from 12 — the months stayed and the months waited are only equal when the year splits in half. Answer: after 6 months
Q3. A and B invest Rs 30,000 and Rs 50,000 for a year. A manages the business and receives one-fifth of the profit for this, the rest being divided in the ratio of the capitals. If the total profit is Rs 20,000, how much does A receive in all?
The management share comes off first: one-fifth of Rs 20,000 is Rs 4,000, leaving Rs 16,000 to divide. The capitals 30,000 : 50,000 reduce to 3 : 5, so A takes three-eighths of Rs 16,000, that is Rs 6,000. His total is 4,000 + 6,000. B receives only his capital part, Rs 10,000, and the two amounts add back to Rs 20,000 as a check. Splitting the full Rs 20,000 in 3 : 5 would give Rs 7,500 — the standard wrong option. Answer: Rs 10,000
Q4. A and B rent a field. A grazes 20 cows for 4 months, while B grazes 30 sheep for 6 months and 20 goats for 4 months. Two cows eat as much as five sheep, and two goats as much as three sheep. What fraction of the rent should A pay?
Bring every animal to one unit first. One cow counts as 5/2 sheep, so A's 20 cows are 50 sheep for 4 months, that is 200 sheep-months. One goat counts as 3/2 sheep, so B's goats are 30 sheep for 4 months, 120 sheep-months, and his sheep give 30 × 6 = 180, making 300 in all. A's fraction is 200 / 500. Converting everything to goats or cows instead gives the same fraction, since only the ratio matters. Answer: two-fifths

How to Prepare Partnership for SSC CGL

  1. Do ratio and proportion first. Partnership is dividing a total in a given ratio, with one extra step in front of it. If ratio is fluent, this chapter takes an evening.
  2. Write the weights before anything else. Put C × t under each partner's name, in thousands if the numbers are large, and reduce. Most questions are finished the moment the weights are on paper.
  3. Count months from each partner's own entry to his own exit. Never assume twelve. A joiner, a leaver and a replacement can all appear in the same question, each with a different count.
  4. Split the year at every change. A partner who alters his capital once gives two stretches, twice gives three. Add capital × months across all of them, and remember that two partners changing at different months cannot share one split point.
  5. Ask what leaves the profit first. Charity, salary, management share — all of it comes off the top, and the capital ratio then applies to the remainder only.
  6. Learn the two partner words. A working partner is paid extra for running the firm; a sleeping partner puts in money only and takes his capital share of what is left.
  7. Work backwards confidently. A single known share fixes everything: one part is that share divided by its own number of parts, and the whole profit is one part times the total. A gap between two shares works the same way, divided by the difference of the parts.
  8. For an equal-share condition, set the products equal. C₁t₁ = C₂t₂ answers every question that asks for the capital, or the month, that makes two partners come out level.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Partnership — Frequently Asked Questions

How many partnership questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually nought to one question in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and about one in Tier II Paper I. The chapter is small but it is cheap to secure, because every question reduces to one rule — capital multiplied by the time it stayed in — and the paper repeats the same eight or ten shapes.
What topics come under partnership for SSC CGL?
Profit divided by capital when the time is the same, capital multiplied by months when it is not, partners joining after a few months or leaving before the year ends, a partner replaced part-way through, capital raised or cut once or twice during the year, a working partner paid a fraction of the profit or a monthly salary, a sleeping partner who takes only his capital share, a fraction set aside for charity before the rest is divided, non-cash contributions such as skilled labour, goods or premises counted as capital, capitals fixed by a total and successive differences, loss shared by the same rule as profit, the capital or the month that makes two shares equal, and rent for a pasture shared by animal-months with an equivalence between animals.
What is the basic formula for partnership questions?
Profit is divided in the ratio of capital multiplied by time. If the partners put in C₁, C₂, C₃ for t₁, t₂, t₃ months, the shares are in the ratio C₁t₁ : C₂t₂ : C₃t₃, and a partner's own share is his weight divided by the total weight, times the profit. When everybody's money stays in for the same length of time the times cancel and the ratio is simply the ratio of the capitals.
What is the difference between a working partner and a sleeping partner?
A working partner runs the business and is paid something extra for it — either a fraction of the profit or a fixed salary — and that payment is taken out before anything is divided. A sleeping partner only puts in money, so he receives his capital share of whatever is left. The commonest slip in these questions is dividing the whole profit by the capitals instead of dividing only the remainder.
How do I handle a partner who joins after a few months?
Count the months his money was actually in the firm and use that number, not twelve. A partner who joins four months after the start is in for eight months, and a partner who leaves at the end of the ninth month is in for nine. Each partner's weight is his own capital times his own months, so one year can hold three different counts.
