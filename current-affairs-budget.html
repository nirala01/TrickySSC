Budget 2026Key highlights

Budget day at a glance

  • Presented – Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman tabled the Union Budget 2026-27 in the Lok Sabha on 1 February 2026.
  • Sunday first – it was the first Union Budget of independent India to be presented on a Sunday.
  • Record run – her ninth consecutive Budget, and the second full Budget of the third Modi government.
  • New home – the first Budget drafted in Kartavya Bhawan; it was billed as a 'Yuva Shakti'-driven Budget.
  • Speech – ran about 1 hour 25 minutes and opened by recalling Magha Purnima and the birth anniversary of Guru Ravidas.
Union Budget: the Constitution calls it the 'Annual Financial Statement' (Article 112); it has been presented on 1 February since 2017, the year the Railway Budget was merged into it; tax changes travel in the Finance Bill.

Three duties behind the Budget

  • First Kartavya – speed up and sustain growth by lifting productivity, competitiveness and resilience to global shocks.
  • Second Kartavya – meet people's aspirations and build their skills so they share in prosperity.
  • Third Kartavya – Sabka Saath, Sabka Vikas: fair access to resources and opportunity for every family, region and sector.
  • Sankalp – the government's stated resolve to put the poor, the underprivileged and the disadvantaged first.
  • Reform Express – more than 350 reforms cited since August 2025, such as simpler GST, the Labour Codes and fewer Quality Control Orders.

Size of the Budget – BE 2026-27

  • Total expenditure – ₹53.47 lakh crore (Budget Estimate), about 7.7% above RE 2025-26.
  • Non-debt receipts – about ₹36.5 lakh crore; net tax to the Centre about ₹28.7 lakh crore.
  • Revenue receipts – ₹35.3 lakh crore, of which non-tax revenue is about ₹6.7 lakh crore.
  • Capital receipts – ₹18.1 lakh crore, mostly borrowing; non-debt capital receipts only about ₹1.2 lakh crore.
  • GDP base – nominal GDP for 2026-27 taken at about ₹393 lakh crore, i.e. roughly 10% nominal growth.

Revised figures for 2025-26 (RE)

  • Spending – total expenditure revised to about ₹49.6 lakh crore.
  • Receipts – non-debt receipts about ₹34 lakh crore; net tax about ₹26.7 lakh crore.
  • Capex – capital spending revised to about ₹11 lakh crore (₹10.96 lakh crore), against a BE of ₹11.2 lakh crore.
  • Deficit – fiscal deficit held at 4.4% of GDP, exactly the Budget Estimate.
  • Promise kept – the pledge to bring the deficit below 4.5% by 2025-26 was met.

Deficit targets for 2026-27 (BE)

  • Fiscal deficit – 4.3% of GDP, or about ₹16.96 lakh crore.
  • Revenue deficit – 1.5% of GDP (about ₹5.92 lakh crore), the same ratio as RE 2025-26.
  • Primary deficit – 0.7% of GDP (about ₹2.92 lakh crore), down from 0.8% in RE 2025-26.
  • Effective revenue deficit – only 0.3% of GDP.
  • New compass – the Centre now steers by a debt-to-GDP goal rather than a single yearly deficit number.
Deficit terms: Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts other than borrowings; Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts; Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments; Effective revenue deficit = revenue deficit − grants for creating capital assets.

Debt and borrowing

  • Debt ratio – Central debt put at 55.6% of GDP for 2026-27 (BE), against 56.1% in RE 2025-26.
  • Anchor – goal of bringing debt down to 50 ± 1% of GDP by 2030-31.
  • Gross borrowing – market borrowing through dated securities of about ₹17.2 lakh crore.
  • Net borrowing – net dated-securities borrowing of about ₹11.7 lakh crore.
  • Interest bill – about ₹14.04 lakh crore, roughly 26% of total spending and 40% of revenue receipts.
FRBM Act: the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, gives the legal frame for deficit and debt targets and requires fiscal policy statements to be laid with the Budget.

Capital spending

  • Public capex – raised to ₹12.2 lakh crore (₹12.22 lakh crore) in BE 2026-27.
  • Year before – BE 2025-26 had provided ₹11.2 lakh crore.
  • Decade jump – up from roughly ₹2 lakh crore in 2014-15.
  • Effective capex – ₹17.1 lakh crore, about 4.4% of GDP, once grants to states for building assets are added.
  • Revenue side – revenue expenditure budgeted at about ₹41.3 lakh crore.
Effective capital expenditure: capital expenditure plus grants-in-aid given for creation of capital assets – a fuller measure of asset-building spending by the Centre.

Where each rupee comes from

  • Borrowings and other liabilities – 24 paise, the largest single source.
  • Income tax – 21 paise; corporation tax – 18 paise.
  • GST and other taxes – 15 paise; union excise duties 6 paise; customs 4 paise.
  • Non-tax revenue – 10 paise; non-debt capital receipts such as disinvestment – 2 paise.
  • All taxes together – about 64 paise of every rupee.

Where each rupee goes

  • States' share of taxes – 22 paise, the biggest outgo.
  • Interest payments – 20 paise.
  • Central sector schemes – 17 paise; defence – 11 paise.
  • Centrally sponsored schemes – 8 paise; Finance Commission and other transfers – 7 paise.
  • Others – major subsidies 6 paise, civil pensions 2 paise and other spending 7 paise.

Finance Commission and states

  • Vertical share – the Centre accepted the 16th Finance Commission advice to keep the states' share of the divisible pool at 41%.
  • FC grants – ₹1.4 lakh crore for 2026-27 for rural and urban local bodies and disaster management.
  • Total transfers – money flowing to states and UTs estimated at about ₹26.2 lakh crore in BE 2026-27.
  • Award period – the 16th FC award runs from 2026-27 to 2030-31.
16th Finance Commission: set up under Article 280 on 31 December 2023; chaired by Arvind Panagariya; its report covers the five years from 1 April 2026.

Defence gets a record sum

  • Allocation – Ministry of Defence gets ₹7.85 lakh crore, its highest ever and the largest for any ministry.
  • Rise – up about 15.19% on BE 2025-26 (₹6.81 lakh crore); about 14.67% of the whole Budget.
  • Capital head – about ₹2.19 lakh crore for the defence forces, nearly 21.84% higher.
  • Buy Indian – about ₹1.39 lakh crore (around 75% of capital acquisition) kept for domestic industry.
  • Research – DRDO's share raised to about ₹29,100 crore; the first Budget after Operation Sindoor.

Spending by major heads (BE)

  • Transport – tops the item-wise list at about ₹5.99 lakh crore.
  • Rural development – about ₹2.73 lakh crore; Home Affairs about ₹2.55 lakh crore.
  • Agriculture and allied – about ₹1.63 lakh crore.
  • Education – about ₹1.39 lakh crore; Health – about ₹1.05 lakh crore.
  • Energy – about ₹1.09 lakh crore; Development of North East – about ₹6,812 crore.

Biopharma push

  • Seven sectors – factories to be scaled up in seven strategic and frontier areas, from biopharma and chips to textiles and sports goods.
  • Biopharma SHAKTI – ₹10,000 crore over five years to build a home base for biologics and biosimilars.
  • Institutes – 3 new NIPERs to be set up and 7 existing ones upgraded.
  • Trials – a network of more than 1,000 accredited clinical trial sites.
  • Regulator – CDSCO to get a dedicated scientific review cadre for quicker approvals.
Biopharma SHAKTI: Amount – ₹10,000 crore over 5 years; Purpose – make India a global biopharma manufacturing hub; Sector – health and pharma; Full form – Strategy for Healthcare Advancement through Knowledge, Technology and Innovation.

Chips and electronics

  • ISM 2.0 – second phase of the India Semiconductor Mission, covering chip-making equipment, materials and full-stack Indian IP.
  • Supply chains – ISM 2.0 also targets secure supply chains and industry-led research and training centres.
  • ECMS boost – outlay of the Electronics Components Manufacturing Scheme lifted from ₹22,919 crore to ₹40,000 crore.
  • ECMS start – the scheme was first launched in April 2025.
India Semiconductor Mission: first phase approved in December 2021 with ₹76,000 crore; runs under the Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY).

Rare earths, chemicals and machines

  • Rare Earth Corridors – in Odisha, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu for mining, processing, research and magnet making.
  • Base scheme – builds on the Rare Earth Permanent Magnet scheme cleared in November 2025.
  • Chemical Parks – 3 parks to be chosen by challenge, on a plug-and-play cluster model.
  • Tool rooms – 2 hi-tech tool rooms to be run by CPSEs.
  • CIE scheme – support for Indian-made construction and infrastructure equipment such as tunnel-boring machines, lifts and fire-fighting gear.

Containers, textiles and crafts

  • Containers – a Container Manufacturing scheme of ₹10,000 crore over five years.
  • Integrated Textile Programme – five parts: National Fibre Scheme, Textile Expansion and Employment Scheme, National Handloom and Handicraft programme, Tex-Eco initiative and Samarth 2.0.
  • Mega Textile Parks – to be picked in challenge mode, with a tilt towards technical textiles.
  • Mahatma Gandhi Gram Swaraj – initiative for khadi, handloom and handicrafts with branding and global links.
  • Sports goods – a special drive for affordable sports goods making, R&D and new materials.

Old clusters and MSMEs

  • Legacy clusters – a scheme to revive 200 old industrial clusters.
  • SME Growth Fund – a dedicated ₹10,000 crore fund for equity support.
  • Top-up – ₹2,000 crore more for the Self-Reliant India Fund of 2021.
  • Corporate Mitras – trained para-professionals from ICAI, ICSI and ICMAI to help MSMEs in Tier II and III towns with compliance.
  • Courier exports – the ₹10 lakh per-consignment value cap scrapped.

TReDS for faster payments

  • CPSE rule – TReDS made compulsory for every CPSE purchase from MSMEs, a model for private buyers.
  • Guarantee – a CGTMSE credit guarantee for invoice discounting on TReDS.
  • GeM link – GeM to be tied to TReDS for cheaper and quicker money.
  • Securitisation – TReDS dues to be packaged as asset-backed securities to create a secondary market.
  • So far – more than ₹7 lakh crore has already reached MSMEs through TReDS.
TReDS: Trade Receivables electronic Discounting System – an RBI-regulated online platform where MSME bills on large buyers are discounted by banks and NBFCs.

Freight, finance and the east

  • Freight – a new Dedicated Freight Corridor from Dankuni (West Bengal) in the east to Surat (Gujarat) in the west.
  • Risk fund – an Infrastructure Risk Guarantee Fund to offer partial credit guarantees to lenders.
  • CPSE REITs – dedicated REITs to recycle land and buildings owned by CPSEs.
  • Cities – infrastructure push continues in cities with more than 5 lakh people.
  • Purvodaya – an East Coast Industrial Corridor with a node at Durgapur, plus 4,000 e-buses for eastern states.

High-speed rail 'growth connectors'

  • Seven corridors – 7 high-speed rail links proposed as green passenger systems between cities.
  • West and south – Mumbai–Pune, Pune–Hyderabad and Hyderabad–Bengaluru.
  • South – Hyderabad–Chennai and Chennai–Bengaluru.
  • North and east – Delhi–Varanasi and Varanasi–Siliguri.
Mumbai–Ahmedabad HSR: India's first bullet-train project, about 508 km long, built by NHSRCL with Japanese Shinkansen technology and JICA loans.

Waterways and coastal shipping

  • National Waterways – 20 new NWs over five years linking mineral belts, industrial hubs and ports.
  • First up – NW-5 in Odisha, joining Talcher and Angul with Kalinga Nagar, Paradeep and Dhamra.
  • Ship repair – repair yards for inland vessels at Varanasi and Patna.
  • Coastal Cargo Promotion Scheme – lift the combined share of river and coastal shipping in cargo to 12% by 2047, double the present 6%.
  • Seaplanes – a Seaplane VGF scheme and incentives to build seaplanes in India.
IWAI: Inland Waterways Authority of India, set up in 1986, HQ Noida; works under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways; the 2016 Act declared 111 National Waterways.

City Economic Regions

  • Idea – map City Economic Regions (CERs) around their growth drivers to tap the power of agglomeration.
  • Money – ₹5,000 crore per CER over five years.
  • Method – challenge mode with reform-cum-results based financing.
  • Focus – temple towns and smaller Tier II and III cities.
  • Municipal bonds – a ₹100 crore reward for a single bond issue above ₹1,000 crore; the AMRUT reward for smaller issues stays.

Energy security

  • CCUS – ₹20,000 crore over five years for Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage in power, steel, cement, refineries and chemicals.
  • Nuclear – customs relief on goods for nuclear power projects stretched to 2035 and opened to plants of every size.
  • Solar glass – sodium antimonate imports made duty-free.
  • Batteries – duty relief on machinery for lithium-ion cells widened to cells for battery energy storage.
  • Green gas – the full value of biogas left out when excise is charged on biogas-blended CNG.

Banking and markets

  • Banking panel – a High Level Committee on Banking for Viksit Bharat.
  • PSU NBFCs – restructuring of Power Finance Corporation (PFC) and REC as a first step.
  • FEMA – a full review of the FEMA (Non-debt Instruments) Rules.
  • Bond market – a market-making framework and total return swaps on corporate bonds.
  • PROIs – individuals living abroad may buy listed Indian shares through the Portfolio Investment Scheme; per-person limit up from 5% to 10%, overall cap from 10% to 24%.

Services, health and care

  • Standing Committee – a powerful panel named 'Education to Employment and Enterprise' to make services a core growth engine, eyeing a 10% share of global services by 2047.
  • Allied health – 1 lakh more allied health professionals in five years across 10 disciplines.
  • Caregivers – 1.5 lakh multi-skilled caregivers to be trained under NSQF courses for elderly care.
  • Medical tourism – 5 regional medical hubs with states and the private sector.
  • Vets – capital subsidy for private veterinary colleges, hospitals and labs to add over 20,000 vet professionals.

AYUSH, mental health and emergencies

  • Ayurveda – three more All India Institutes of Ayurveda.
  • AYUSH quality – drug-testing labs and pharmacies under AYUSH to be modernised.
  • WHO centre – upgrade of the WHO traditional medicine centre at Jamnagar.
  • Mental health – NIMHANS-2 for north India; institutes at Ranchi and Tezpur to become regional apex institutions.
  • Emergency care – emergency and trauma centres in district hospitals, lifting their capacity by about 50%.
WHO GCTM: the WHO Global Centre for Traditional Medicine at Jamnagar, Gujarat – the first global outposted centre of its kind; foundation stone laid in April 2022.

Education and the orange economy

  • AVGC labs – labs for young content creators in 15,000 secondary schools plus 500 colleges, with the Indian Institute of Creative Technology, Mumbai.
  • NID – one more National Institute of Design for eastern India via the challenge route.
  • University Townships – 5 near major industrial and logistics corridors.
  • Girls' hostels – one girls' hostel in every district for higher-education STEM institutions.
  • Telescopes – 4 facilities: National Large Solar Telescope, National Large Optical-infrared Telescope, Himalayan Chandra Telescope and COSMOS-2 planetarium.
Orange economy: the creative economy – animation, visual effects, gaming and comics (AVGC), films, music and design; the AVGC sector is expected to need about 2 million professionals by 2030.

Tourism and heritage

  • Hospitality – the NCHMCT to be turned into a National Institute of Hospitality.
  • Guides – pilot to upskill 10,000 guides at 20 iconic sites through a 12-week hybrid course with an IIM.
  • Digital grid – a National Destination Digital Knowledge Grid that will digitally map every notable place.
  • Heritage – 15 archaeological sites, including Lothal, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Adichanallur, Sarnath, Hastinapur and Leh Palace, to become visitor hubs.
  • Buddhist Circuits – in Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram and Tripura.

Nature trails and big cats

  • Mountain trails – in Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Jammu and Kashmir, Araku Valley and Podhigai Malai.
  • Turtle trails – along the coasts of Odisha, Karnataka and Kerala.
  • Bird trails – bird-watching trails around Pulicat Lake.
  • Big Cat Summit – India to host the first Global Big Cat Summit in 2026 for leaders of 95 range countries.
International Big Cat Alliance (IBCA): launched by PM Modi on 9 April 2023 to mark 50 years of Project Tiger; covers seven big cats – tiger, lion, leopard, snow leopard, puma, jaguar and cheetah.

Sports

  • Khelo India Mission – a 10-year mission to reshape the sports system.
  • Five parts – talent pathway, coach development, sports science and technology, competitions and leagues, and sports infrastructure.
  • Industry – the sports goods initiative backs low-cost equipment and research.
  • Ministry – Khelo India is run by the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports.

Farmers and fisheries

  • Fisheries – integrated development of 500 reservoirs and Amrit Sarovars, with coastal value chains and fish FPOs.
  • High-value crops – coconut, sandalwood, cocoa and cashew on the coasts; agar trees in the North East; almond, walnut and pine nut in the hills.
  • Coconut Promotion Scheme – replace old, unproductive trees to raise output.
  • Cashew and cocoa – a dedicated programme to build premium global Indian brands by 2030.
  • Animal husbandry – credit-linked subsidy and livestock FPOs for dairy, poultry and livestock value chains.
Bharat-VISTAAR: Virtually Integrated System to Access Agricultural Resources – a multilingual AI advisory tool joining AgriStack portals with ICAR farm practices; about ₹150 crore set aside for 2026-27.

Women and Divyangjan

  • SHE-Marts – Self-Help Entrepreneur Marts: community-owned shops run by women's cluster federations, building on Lakhpati Didi.
  • Divyangjan Kaushal Yojana – skill training for jobs in IT, AVGC, hospitality and food and beverage.
  • Divyang Sahara Yojana – quick access to good assistive devices for all who qualify.
  • ALIMCO – support to scale up output, R&D and AI use; PM Divyasha Kendras to become modern retail-style centres.
ALIMCO: Artificial Limbs Manufacturing Corporation of India – a CPSE under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, based in Kanpur, set up in 1972.

Income-tax basics

  • New law – the Income-tax Act, 2025 comes into force on 1 April 2026, replacing the 1961 Act.
  • Slabs – no change in personal income-tax slabs or rates under either regime.
  • New regime – nil up to ₹4 lakh, then 5% to 30%; the 30% rate applies above ₹24 lakh.
  • Return dates – ITR-1 and ITR-2 stay at 31 July; non-audit business cases and trusts get till 31 August.
  • Revised returns – can be filed till 31 March (earlier 31 December) on paying a small fee.

Relief for individuals

  • MACT interest – interest awarded by a Motor Accident Claims Tribunal to a person made tax-free, with no TDS.
  • Foreign tours – TCS on overseas tour packages cut to a flat 2% (from 5% and 20%).
  • LRS – TCS on money sent abroad for education and medical care cut from 5% to 2%.
  • NRI property – TDS on property bought from a non-resident can be paid with the buyer's PAN; no TAN needed.
  • Forms – depositories can accept Form 15G/15H from investors holding shares in many companies.

Penalties, prosecution and disclosure

  • FAST-DS 2026 – Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers Disclosure Scheme: a one-time, six-month window for small overseas assets or income.
  • Immunity – failing to report movable foreign assets worth under ₹20 lakh freed from prosecution, with effect from 1 October 2024.
  • Jail terms – maximum imprisonment for tax offences cut to 2 years; minor ones made fine-only.
  • Appeals – pre-deposit for the first appeal cut from 20% to 10% of the core demand.
  • Updated returns – allowed even after reassessment starts, at 10% extra tax.
FAST-DS 2026: Category A – undisclosed foreign income or assets up to ₹1 crore (30% tax plus an equal levy); Category B – assets up to ₹5 crore (flat ₹1 lakh fee); window opened 16 August 2026 and runs till 31 December 2026.

Markets and company tax

  • STT on futures – now 0.05% (earlier 0.02%).
  • STT on options – premium from 0.1% and exercise from 0.125%, both to 0.15%.
  • Buyback – taxed as capital gains for every shareholder; promoters pay extra (effective 22% for companies, 30% for others).
  • MAT – becomes a final tax at 14% (down from 15%); no fresh MAT credit from 1 April 2026.
  • MAT credit – old credit may offset up to one-fourth of tax in the new regime; non-residents on presumptive tax freed from MAT.

IT services, data centres and global firms

  • Safe harbour – IT services grouped into one category with a common 15.5% margin.
  • Threshold – safe harbour limit raised from ₹300 crore to ₹2,000 crore, cleared by an automated process and kept for five years.
  • Data centres – tax holiday till 2047 for foreign firms selling cloud services worldwide from Indian data centres.
  • Related entities – Indian data-centre units serving group firms get a 15% cost-plus safe harbour.
  • Experts and toll makers – five-year exemption on foreign income of non-resident experts; five-year exemption for non-residents supplying capital goods to toll makers in bonded zones.

Customs cuts for people and industry

  • Personal imports – duty on goods imported for personal use cut from 20% to 10%.
  • Cancer relief – basic customs duty removed on 17 medicines; 7 more rare diseases added to the personal-import exemption.
  • Rare earth ore – duty on monazite cut from 2.5% to nil.
  • Aircraft – parts for civil and training aircraft, and raw materials for defence MRO, made duty-free.
  • Factories – set parts for microwave ovens and machinery for critical mineral processing exempted.

Export and customs reforms

  • Seafood – duty-free input limit for seafood exporters raised from 1% to 3% of last year's FOB exports.
  • Footwear and garments – export window raised from 6 months to 1 year; duty-free inputs extended to shoe uppers.
  • Deep-sea fish – catch by Indian vessels in the EEZ or high seas made duty-free; landing at a foreign port counts as export.
  • AEO – duty deferral for Authorised Economic Operators in Tiers 2 and 3 raised from 15 to 30 days.
  • Rulings and systems – advance ruling validity up from 3 to 5 years; a Customs Integrated System within 2 years.

Budget words to know

  • BE – Budget Estimate: the projection for the coming year (2026-27 here).
  • RE – Revised Estimate: the mid-year update for the running year (2025-26 here).
  • Actuals – final audited figures of a finished year (2024-25 here).
  • Capital receipts – borrowings, loan recoveries and disinvestment; disinvestment is a non-debt capital receipt.
  • Revenue expenditure – spending that creates no assets, such as interest, salaries, pensions and subsidies.
Disinvestment: sale of government equity in public sector enterprises; handled by DIPAM (Department of Investment and Public Asset Management) in the Finance Ministry.
