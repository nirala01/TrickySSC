Every probability question comes down to one fraction: favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes, where every outcome in the total is equally likely. The skill is listing the sample space correctly before you count — two dice give 36 ordered pairs, not 21; three coins give 8 outcomes, not 4; and a word with repeated letters has as many outcomes as it has letters, not as many different letters.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is counting an overlap twice — “a king or a spade” is 16 cards, not 17, because the king of spades is both. The second is adding when you should multiply: “A and B” for independent events is a product, and “at least one” is almost always fastest as 1 − P(none). The third is ignoring what the question already tells you — once you know the total on two dice is 8, only the 5 pairs that make 8 are left in play. Each test puts twenty-five probability questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those checks automatic.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Treating unequal cases as equal. “0, 1 or 2 heads” are not three equally likely cases — one head happens in two ways out of four. Always count from the full list of outcomes.
  • Forgetting 1 and 0. 1 is not prime but it is a perfect square and a perfect cube; 0 is a digit. Leaving one of them out, or letting one in, moves the answer to a wrong option.
  • Using the old total after a change. When cards are removed or balls are added, both the favourable count and the total change. Recount both before dividing.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Probability Questions

Q1. Two dice are thrown together. What is the probability that the total is 9?
Two dice give 6 × 6 = 36 ordered outcomes. A total of 9 comes from (3,6), (4,5), (5,4) and (6,3) — 4 outcomes. P = 4/36 = 1/9. Answer: 1/9
Q2. A bag has 4 red and 6 blue balls. Two balls are drawn together. What is the probability that both are blue?
All pairs: 10C2 = 45. Blue pairs: 6C2 = 15. P = 15/45 = 1/3. Multiplying 6/10 × 6/10 would be wrong — that treats the first ball as if it went back. Answer: 1/3
Q3. A hits a target with probability 3/5 and B with probability 1/2, independently. Each fires once. What is the probability that the target is hit?
“Hit” means at least one of them hits, so use the complement: both miss with 2/5 × 1/2 = 1/5. P = 1 − 1/5 = 4/5. Answer: 4/5
Q4. A number is picked from 1 to 60. What is the probability that it is divisible by 4 or by 6?
Multiples of 4: 15. Multiples of 6: 10. Both means multiples of LCM(4, 6) = 12: 5. So 15 + 10 − 5 = 20 and P = 20/60 = 1/3. Answer: 1/3

How to Prepare Probability for SSC CGL

  1. Write the sample space size first. 2, 4, 8 or 16 for coins; 6, 36 or 216 for dice; 52 for cards. Getting the denominator right settles half the question.
  2. Count ordered pairs on dice. (1, 2) and (2, 1) are different outcomes. A total of 7 happens in 6 ways, not 3.
  3. Reach for the complement. “At least one”, “neither” and “not” questions are nearly always quicker as 1 − the opposite.
  4. Look for the overlap. Whenever a question says “or”, ask what is counted twice — the king of spades, multiples of the LCM, the number 1.
  5. Know the pack of 52 by heart. 4 suits of 13; 26 red and 26 black; 12 face cards; 4 aces; 36 number cards from 2 to 10.
  6. Multiply for “and”, add for “or” — but only for the right events. Multiply when the events are independent; add when they cannot happen together.
  7. Use what the question tells you. “Given that” or “it is known that” means you count only inside that smaller set.
  8. Check the answer is between 0 and 1. A total above 1 means something was added twice. One check rules out two options.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is counting correctly, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Probability Questions — Frequently Asked Questions

How many probability questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually none or one in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and one or two in Tier II Paper I. They are mostly coins, dice, cards and balls, and each one takes under a minute once the sample space is counted correctly.
What topics come under probability for SSC CGL?
Valid probability values and the complement rule, coins, dice, the pack of 52 cards, balls and cards drawn with or without replacement, numbered tickets with squares, primes and divisibility, letters of a word, digits, independent events, truth-tellers who agree or contradict, the addition theorem, odds, repeated trials, leap years with 53 Sundays, and committees.
What is the basic formula of probability?
P(E) = number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes. The answer always lies between 0 and 1: 0 for an impossible event, 1 for a certain one, and P(not E) = 1 − P(E).
How do I solve 'at least one' probability questions?
Work out the chance that none of the events happens and subtract it from 1. For three independent shooters who hit with chances 0.9, 0.8 and 0.7, all three miss with 0.1 × 0.2 × 0.3 = 0.006, so at least one hits with 0.994.
What is the difference between mutually exclusive and independent events?
Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so for them P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Independent events do not affect each other's chances, so for them P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Drawing a king and drawing a queen in one draw are mutually exclusive; heads on one coin and a six on a die are independent.
Do I need permutations and combinations for probability in SSC CGL?
Only a little. Questions where two or three balls or cards are taken together, and committee questions, use nCr — for example 10C2 = 45 pairs. Every such solution in the tests explains the count step by step, so the basic formula is enough.
How many questions are in each probability practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the probability tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with probability?
Basics of numbers and divisibility rules supply the primes, squares and multiples that numbered-ticket questions count; LCM and HCF gives the overlap in 'divisible by a or b'; fractions and ratio help simplify answers; and statistics is usually studied in the same block.
