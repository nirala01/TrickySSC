Profit and Loss is the chapter SSC returns to in almost every shift, and it is the one most candidates think they already know. The formulas are short and the arithmetic is light. What actually decides the mark is something narrower: knowing which number goes underneath. A profit percent sits on the cost price, a discount sits on the marked price, and a dealer who quotes his margin on what he received is quoting a different figure from one who quotes it on what he spent.

What You Will Practice in the Profit & Loss Chapter

Core topics

  • Cost price, selling price and marked price — and which of the three a given rate is measured on
  • Gain and loss percent from two plain figures, and the reverse: finding the cost from the selling price
  • Overheads, carriage, repairs and commission — what joins the cost and what comes off the takings
  • Successive deals, where the proceeds of one sale become the cost of the next
  • Goods passing through two or three hands, each taking a margin
  • Two articles sold at the same price, one at a gain and one at a loss
  • Gain or loss expressed as the cost price or selling price of some of the articles
  • Part of a consignment sold at one rate and the rest at another
  • Spoilage and wastage — stock that is paid for but never earns
  • False weights, short measures and dishonest dealers
  • Profit reckoned on the selling price instead of the cost price
  • Free-goods offers such as "buy 5, get 1 free"

Formulas worth memorising

SituationFormula
Profit percentP% = (SP − CP) / CP × 100
Loss percentL% = (CP − SP) / CP × 100
Selling price from costSP = CP × (100 + P) / 100
Cost price from selling priceCP = SP × 100 / (100 + P)
Two prices, same costSP₁ − SP₂ = CP × (rate gap) / 100
Equal gain and loss on one pricenet loss = x² / 100 % of total CP
Short weight, sold at costgain% = (1000 − w) / w × 100
Profit as a share of the takingsrate on SP = P / (100 + P) × 100

In the rate-gap row, the two rates are subtracted when both prices lie on the same side of the cost, and added when one is a gain and the other a loss.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Dividing by the wrong figure. A profit percent always sits on the cost. Dividing the profit by the selling price gives a smaller number that is not wrong arithmetic but is not what was asked.
  • Adding rates that should multiply. A gain of 20% followed by a loss of 20% is not a break-even — it is a loss of 4%, because the second rate acts on a larger amount than the first.
  • Forgetting the stock that earns nothing. Spoiled goods, free samples and the items given away under an offer all cost money and bring none in, so they belong in the cost and not in the takings.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Profit & Loss

Q1. An article sold for Rs 4,140 gives the seller a profit of 15%. At what price must it be sold to make a profit of 32%?
Come back to the cost first — a new rate can never be applied to an old selling price. CP = 4140 × 100 / 115 = 3600. Then SP = 3600 × 132 / 100. Answer: Rs 4,752
Q2. Two articles are sold at the same price, one at a gain of 20% and the other at a loss of 20%. What is the result?
Equal rates on a common price never cancel. The pair always loses x² / 100 percent of the total cost, so here the loss is 400 / 100 = 4%. The article sold at a loss is the dearer of the two, which is why the deal ends behind. Answer: a loss of 4%
Q3. A shopkeeper sells at the cost price but uses a weight of 900 g for each kilogram he charges for. What is his gain percent?
Take the goods at Re 1 per gram. The customer pays 1000 and carries away 900 g, which cost the shopkeeper 900. Gain = 100 / 900 × 100 = 11.11%. Note the denominator: the gain is measured against what left the shop, not what was billed. Answer: 11.11%
Q4. A dealer's gain on selling 84 chairs equals the cost price of 12 chairs. What is his gain percent?
Let each chair cost Re 1. The 84 chairs cost 84 and the gain is 12, so the rate is 12 / 84 × 100 = 142⁄7%. Had the gain been worth 12 chairs at their selling price, the answer would have been 162⁄3% instead. Answer: 142⁄7%

How to Prepare Profit and Loss for SSC CGL

  1. Do percentage first. Every formula in this chapter is a percentage with a different name on it. If percentage is shaky, profit and loss will feel like memorising eight separate rules instead of applying one.
  2. Before you divide, say out loud what the base is. Cost price for a profit percent, marked price for a discount, selling price only when the question names it. More marks are lost here than to any calculation error.
  3. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right base and the right formula, not speed.
  4. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. A Tier I profit and loss question that takes more than about forty-five seconds usually means you are solving it the long way round.
  5. Learn the two standing results. The equal-price pair losing x²/100 percent, and the short-weight gain of (1000 − w)/w — between them they cover a good share of the harder questions and take seconds once known.
  6. Read the solution even for questions you got right. The shortcut in the solution panel is often two steps shorter than the method you used.
  7. Verify against a real paper. Once Level 1 is consistent under the clock, attempt a previous year paper and then a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Profit & Loss — Frequently Asked Questions

How many profit and loss questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually two to three questions in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and three to four in Tier II Paper I. Taken with the chapters it shares its formulas with — percentage, discount and simple interest — the block is worth five to seven marks in most shifts.
What topics come under profit and loss for SSC CGL?
Cost price and selling price, gain and loss percent, finding the cost price from the selling price, overheads and carriage, successive deals, two articles sold at the same price, gain expressed as the price of some of the articles, false weights and short measures, dishonest dealers combining a markup with a discount and a false weight, mixtures, and profit measured on the selling price instead of the cost.
What is the formula for profit and loss percent?
Profit percent is (SP − CP) ÷ CP × 100, and loss percent is (CP − SP) ÷ CP × 100. The denominator is always the cost price. Going the other way, SP = CP × (100 + P) ÷ 100 and CP = SP × 100 ÷ (100 + P).
If two articles are sold at the same price, one at a gain of x% and the other at a loss of x%, what is the result?
There is always a loss, never a break-even, and it comes to x²/100 percent of the total cost price. At 10% either way the pair loses 1%, at 20% it loses 4%. The article sold at a loss is always the dearer of the two, which is why the two rates cannot cancel.
