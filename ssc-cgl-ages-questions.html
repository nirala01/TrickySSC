Problems on ages look like a dozen different question types, but they run on two facts. The first: the gap between two ages never changes, because both people gain the same number of years — so a father who is 30 years above his son is 30 years above him at every moment in the question, and that gap is exactly his age when the son was born. The second: a ratio of ages does change, and it always drifts towards 1 : 1 as time passes and away from it as you go back.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is carrying a ratio across time — if two ages are 8 : 3 today they are not 8 : 3 in ten years, yet the unchanged ratio is on the paper almost every time. The second is moving a total the wrong way: two people gain 2 years of combined age each year, three people gain 3, so a sum of 60 today was 50 five years ago, not 55. The third is answering at the wrong moment — solving correctly and then reporting today’s age when the question named a year in the future. Each test puts twenty-five ages questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those checks automatic.

Two rows carry the chapter: the gap never changes, and a ratio always does. Most of the others are those two applied in a particular order.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Keeping a ratio fixed across time. Ages of 8 : 3 today become 48 : 18, that is 8 : 3, only if no time passes; ten years later the same pair stands at 58 : 28, which is 29 : 14. Age both sides, then reduce.
  • Moving a total by k instead of 2k. Two people who total 60 today totalled 60 − 10 = 50 five years ago, because each of them was five years younger. With three people the total moves by 3k.
  • Answering at the wrong moment. Many questions solve at one moment and ask at another — the father’s age today when you have just found it for four years hence, or the son’s when the question named the father. Underline what is asked before writing the answer.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Problems on Ages

Q1. The ages of a father and his son are in the ratio 4 : 1 and the product of their present ages is 400. What is the father’s present age?
Write the ages as 4x and x. Their product is 4x² = 400, so x² = 100 and x = 10. The father is 4 × 10 = 40 and the son 10. Check: 40 × 10 = 400 and 40 : 10 = 4 : 1. The multiplier 10 is not an age, which is the usual slip here. Answer: 40 years
Q2. The present ages of a man and his son are in the ratio 5 : 2. Eight years from now the man will be twice as old as his son. Find their present ages.
Ages are 5x and 2x. In eight years: 5x + 8 = 2(2x + 8), so 5x + 8 = 4x + 16 and x = 8. The man is 40 and the son 16. Check: in eight years they are 48 and 24, and 48 is twice 24. Answer: 40 and 16 years
Q3. A father is three times as old as his son. Ten years from now he will be twice as old as his son. How old was the father when his son was born?
Ages are 3s and s. In ten years: 3s + 10 = 2(s + 10), so s = 10 and the father is 30. The gap is 30 − 10 = 20 years, and a gap never changes, so the father was 20 when his son was born. Note the short cut: when the elder is twice the younger, the younger’s age equals the gap, so that moment arrives when the son is 20. Answer: 20 years
Q4. The sum of the present ages of a father and his son is 56 years. Four years ago the father was 5 times as old as his son. What is the son’s present age?
Four years ago the two of them totalled 56 − 8 = 48 years — eight less, not four, because both were younger. That total splits as 5 : 1, which is 6 parts, so one part is 8: the son was 8 and the father 40. Today they are 12 and 44, adding to 56. Answer: 12 years

How to Prepare Problems on Ages for SSC CGL

  1. Write the gap down first. It is the one quantity time cannot touch, and it is also the parent’s age at the child’s birth.
  2. Put the unknown multiplier on the moment the ratio belongs to. If the ratio is given for today, the ages are ax and bx today; if it is given for a past year, they are the past ages.
  3. Add or subtract the years from every age. Never from one side only, and never from the ratio numbers.
  4. Move totals by 2k for two people and 3k for three. This single check clears most family questions.
  5. Turn a named moment into a number of years. “When the son is 20” or “when the elder turns 60” must become a count of years before either age can be moved.
  6. Convert mixed numbers before you start. Two-and-a-half times means × 5/2; multiply the whole equation by the denominator straight away.
  7. Use the twice rule as a short cut. When the elder is m times the younger, the younger’s age is gap ÷ (m − 1) — and simply the gap when m is 2.
  8. Read ordering sentences before writing anything. “B is older than A” is there to fix which way a difference runs, and both readings can give whole-number ages.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same day you finish the theory. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is picking the right method, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Problems on Ages — Frequently Asked Questions

How many questions on ages are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually one question in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, and one to two in Tier II Paper I. It is a small chapter, but a dependable one: the method is fixed, the arithmetic is light, and a prepared candidate can finish an ages question in well under a minute.
What topics come under problems on ages for SSC CGL?
The ratio of two ages with their product or their sum; ratios given at two different moments, one in the past and one in the future; one age as a multiple or a fractional multiple of another; finding how many years ago or hence a stated ratio holds; a parent's age at a child's birth; three-generation chains; two families joined by a single age gap; children born at equal intervals; a parent's age compared with the total of the children's ages; the self-referential sentence puzzles; and two-digit age questions with a digit condition.
What is the one rule that solves most age questions?
The age gap between two people never changes. Both people gain the same number of years, so the difference is the same today, ten years ago and twenty years from now. A parent's age at a child's birth is exactly that gap, and when one person is twice as old as another, the younger person's age equals the gap.
Why does the ratio of two ages keep changing?
Because equal years are added to both ages, which pulls the ratio towards 1 : 1. A father and son at 8 : 3 today are not at 8 : 3 in ten years' time. Going back in time does the opposite and makes the ratio steeper, which is why a past ratio is always further from 1 : 1 than the present one.
How do I handle a sum of ages at a different moment?
A total belongs to two people, so it rises by 2 years every year and falls by 2 every year you go back. If two people total 60 today, they totalled 50 five years ago, not 55. With three people the total moves by 3 a year, which is where most wrong options in family questions come from.
What does 'times more than' mean in an ages question?
Strictly, five times more than means six times as much, and papers are not always careful with it. Every question in these tests says 'as old as' instead, so nothing turns on the reading; in the exam, if an option matches the 'as old as' reading, that is almost always what was intended.
How many questions are in each problems on ages practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the ages tests available in Hindi?

Which chapters should I study along with problems on ages?
Ratio and proportion first, because almost every ages question is a ratio question with time added to it. Linear equations help with the two-moment questions, and averages is worth keeping separate: family-average questions belong to that chapter, not this one.
