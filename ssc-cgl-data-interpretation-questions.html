A data interpretation question is mostly arithmetic you already know — percentage, ratio and average — applied to numbers you have to read correctly first. The chart gives the data; the question tells you which few numbers matter. The skill is reading exactly what is asked: the right bar, the right year, the right series, and the right base for every percentage.

The marks are lost in three places. The first is the wrong base — “A is what per cent more than B” divides by B, not by A, and a percentage change always divides by the old value. The second is mixing up per cent and percentage points: a share moving from 20% to 30% has risen by 10 percentage points, which is a 50% increase in the share. The third is adding or averaging things that cannot be added — growth rates of groups of different sizes, ratios from different departments, or two successive 10% falls, which make 19% and not 20%. Each test puts twenty-five chart and table questions in front of you in one sitting, which is what makes those checks automatic.

What You Will Practice in the Data Interpretation Chapter

Tables

  • Row and column totals, differences and sums over a named range of years
  • Ratios between two cells, two rows or two column totals
  • A cell as a percentage of its row or of its column, and percentage change between years
  • Averages of a row or a column, a missing entry from a total, an average or a percentage share
  • Derived quantities — price × quantity, pass rates, running stock, aggregate percentages when the maximum marks differ
  • Counting rows that meet one or two conditions, and cumulative (less-than and more-than) frequency tables

Bar and line charts

  • Simple bars and horizontal bars — totals, differences, ranges and one bar as a percentage of another
  • Grouped bars — one series against another, gaps, shares and the category with the best rate
  • Stacked bars — block values, bar totals, shares, and blocks read from running-total labels
  • Line graphs — year-on-year change, the steepest rise or fall, the gap between two lines, and lines that show a growth rate or profit per cent instead of a value
  • Bar-and-line combos — quantity on the bars, rate or price on the line

Pie charts, histograms and caselets

  • Pie charts in per cent and in degrees — a sector’s value, a sector from another sector, and the total from a gap
  • Two pies with different totals — comparing the same head across two years or two groups
  • A pie read with a table — head-counts from the pie, then a ratio or a rate from the table
  • Histograms — counts in a range, shares above a cut-off and the modal class
  • Caselets — data given only in words, including two-way splits and overlapping groups

The first four rows answer most Tier I questions. The base of every percentage is the number after “of” or “than” — circle it before you calculate.

The three mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Dividing by the wrong base. A rise from 250 to 300 is 20% of 250, not 16.7% of 300. “More than B” and “of B” both put B underneath.
  • Confusing per cent with percentage points. A share going from 30% to 36% is up 6 percentage points; a pass rate of 85% against 68% is 25% higher, not 17%.
  • Adding what should be weighted. The average of three schools’ growth rates is not the town’s growth, and 3 : 2 plus 7 : 3 is not 10 : 5. Go back to the actual counts.

Solved Examples — SSC CGL Data Interpretation Questions

Q1. A shop sold 240 fans in 2023 and 300 fans in 2024. By what percentage did sales rise?
Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 = (300 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = 60 ÷ 240 × 100. Dividing by 300 instead would give 20% — the wrong base. Answer: 25%
Q2. A family’s monthly budget of ₹50,000 is shown as a pie chart. The Education sector makes a central angle of 72°. How much goes to Education?
Value = θ ÷ 360 × total = 72 ÷ 360 × 50,000 = ⅕ × 50,000. Answer: ₹10,000
Q3. School A’s enrolment rose from 400 to 460 and school B’s from 600 to 630. By what percentage did their combined enrolment rise?
The schools grew by 15% and 5%, but their plain average (10%) ignores that B is larger. Work on the totals: 1,000 → 1,090, so the rise is 90 ÷ 1,000 × 100. Answer: 9%
Q4. In a pie chart of monthly spending, Food is 30% and Rent is 20%. The family spends ₹4,000 more on Food than on Rent. What is its total monthly spending?
The gap is 30 − 20 = 10 percentage points, worth ₹4,000, so 1% = ₹400 and the total is 100 × 400. Answer: ₹40,000

How to Prepare Data Interpretation for SSC CGL

  1. Get percentage, ratio and average fast first. Data interpretation adds no new maths; it only tests how quickly and cleanly you apply these three.
  2. Read the question before the chart. Know which year, series and operation you need, then pick only those numbers off the chart.
  3. Check the units and the scale. Thousands against lakhs, crores against lakhs, and per cent against degrees change the answer by a factor.
  4. Circle the base of every percentage. It is the number after “of”, “than” or “over the previous year”.
  5. Learn the fraction–percentage table. 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/6 = 16.67%, 1/3 = 33.33% and 3.6° = 1% turn most pie and growth questions into mental maths.
  6. Compare with ratios, not full division. To find the largest percentage, a quick cross-multiplication is often enough.
  7. Never add percentages or ratios across unequal groups. Convert to actual numbers, add those, then convert back.
  8. Watch “more than” and “at least”. Threshold questions are decided by whether a value exactly on the line counts.
  9. Do Level 1 untimed, the same week you revise percentage and ratio. It is set at real SSC CGL difficulty; the aim is reading correctly, not speed.
  10. Repeat Level 1 with the 15-minute clock on, then move to Level 2. After that, verify against a previous year paper and a full mock test under locked sectional timing.

SSC CGL Data Interpretation Questions — Frequently Asked Questions

How many data interpretation questions are asked in SSC CGL?
Usually two to three in Tier I out of the 25 maths questions, most often a small set on one table, bar chart or pie chart, and a few more in Tier II Paper I. Since the arithmetic is simple, they are among the most scoring questions in the section.
Which types of charts come in SSC CGL data interpretation?
Tables, simple, grouped, stacked and horizontal bar charts, line graphs with one or two lines, pie charts in per cent or in degrees, and occasionally histograms, bar-and-line combinations and caselets where the data is given only in words. All of these appear in the practice tests.
Which formulas are most useful for data interpretation?
Percentage share (part ÷ total × 100), percentage change ((new − old) ÷ old × 100), “A is more than B by” ((A − B) ÷ B × 100), a pie sector from degrees (θ ÷ 360 × total, with 3.6° = 1%), averages and weighted averages, successive change ((1 + a)(1 + b) − 1) and profit per cent ((income − expenditure) ÷ expenditure × 100).
What is the difference between per cent and percentage points?
Percentage points measure the gap between two percentages; per cent measures that gap relative to the starting value. A share moving from 20% to 30% has risen by 10 percentage points, which is a 50% increase in the share. SSC options often include both, so read which one is asked.
How do I read a pie chart given in degrees?
The whole pie is 360°, so a sector’s value is its angle ÷ 360 × the total, and 3.6° stands for 1%. Within one pie, sectors compare exactly as their angles do, so a sector can be found from another sector without knowing the total.
Are the charts shown with every question in these tests?
Yes. Fifteen to eighteen questions in every test come with their own clearly labelled chart — bar, line, pie, histogram or combo — drawn for TrickySSC and shown alongside the question in both English and Hindi. Table questions show the table as text inside the question.
How many questions are in each data interpretation practice test?
Twenty-five questions per test, with an optional 15-minute timer and the real exam marking scheme of +2 for a correct answer, −0.5 for a wrong one and 0 for an unattempted question.
Are the data interpretation tests available in Hindi?
Yes. Questions, options, charts, detailed solutions and shortcut tricks are available in Hindi, and you choose English or Hindi before the test starts.
Which chapters should I study along with data interpretation?
Percentage, ratio and proportion, and averages supply almost all of the arithmetic; profit and loss explains the income, expenditure and profit-per-cent charts; and statistics covers the histograms, cumulative tables and averages of grouped data.
